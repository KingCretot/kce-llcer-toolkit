Week 9 — Tutor Guide

Brooklyn exam preparation, then the dossier workshop

Ask Justine first

Five questions to settle in the first two minutes
  1. Has your teacher shown you a past paper or described the exam? How many questions? Marks? Any passage?
  2. Is the exam short answers, longer answers, or both?
  3. On the cover page, which rows does your teacher say must be filled, and how many documents in each?
  4. Which documents did your group present for Kahlo and for Sherman? (The Digipad suggests the Trotsky portrait and Untitled Film Still #12 for her group; confirm.)
  5. Why did you choose Rockwell? (Do not supply a reason. If she has none, that is the work for Part 2.)

If her answers change the plan (for example, the exam is mostly one long essay), shift the Question Lab toward longer answers and keep the rest.

How the lesson works on screen

The lesson is a screen-by-screen deck: one idea per screen, big type, Next and Back buttons (arrow keys also work). Nothing scrolls in normal use. The Part 1 and Part 2 tabs at the top jump between the two parts. Part 1 has 12 screens, then a gold break screen. In Part 2, the dossier cover page sits on the right of the screen and fills in as you go. By the last screen it is complete, and you can print it or save it as a PDF, or copy it as text. Justine types nothing; you click and, for her central question, you type what she says.

Part 1

Run sheet: Brooklyn, the written exam

Screens 1–2: Today and the paper (3 min)

Read the three facts aloud. Ask her what she thinks the exam will look like. The goal is for her to name the three skills: story, question reading, answer building.

Screens 3–8: the six movements (15 min)

For each movement: the keywords are on screen. Ask her to say the movement aloud in two sentences. Then ask the question on screen and let her answer. Click Show answer to compare, then Show proof from the book: it lists the actual moments from the story that back the answer, and a line saying which exam topics they serve. Ask her to say the moments back to you without looking.

What to listen for
  • Names are right (Father Flood, Mrs. Kehoe, Miss Fortini, Georgina, Miss Kelly, Jim Farrell, Frank).
  • The order of events is right, especially the chain in movements 5 and 6: Rose dies, Jack writes, Eilis marries Tony, goes home, postpones her return for Nancy's wedding, meets Jim, Jim raises an engagement after the wedding, Miss Kelly reveals she knows, Eilis acts.
  • She explains why, not only what. If she lists events, ask "and why does that matter?"
Facts used (from the study-guide beats, not from the novel)

The plot facts on the student page come from the beats in her Brooklyn notes. If her edition differs on a detail, her book wins. These are the ones most worth a quick check against her copy: who tells Eilis about Rose's death (the beats say Father Flood, at Bartocci's, then her mother phones); who paid her second-year tuition (the beats say "a parishioner"); and the exact order in the Enniscorthy chapters (the beats put Jim before the wedding, his engagement talk after it, then Miss Kelly). The student pages also say the book is set in the early 1950s and was published in 2009; both are widely stated but are not in her notes.

Screen 9: Read the question (6 min)

Pick questions 1 to 4 with the buttons. For each one: she says the job (command word) and you click Show the job; then she answers the question aloud; only then you click Show a good answer. The good answers use moments from the book and are samples, not scripts; accept any answer built on accurate moments.

If she misses the difference between explain and how does the author show, say: "Explain asks for reasons. How does the author show asks for his choices: what he says, what he leaves out, how he tells it."

Screen 10: Build an answer (8 min)

This is the heart of Part 1. Each tile shows what the move is. Ask her to say her own Point, then click the tile to fill in the blank with the model (Point, Evidence from each home, Explain, Link). Move through all four, then make her say the whole paragraph aloud and listen against the four moves. Her moments may differ from the model (for example, her mother's pride and Jim for Enniscorthy, Rose's death and Jack's letter): accept any accurate pair of moments.

Another strong version, to compare with the model on screen (do not read to her)

Point: "Tóibín shows that Eilis is torn between two homes by letting each place hold part of her, so that she cannot fully choose either."
Evidence: "When she goes back to Enniscorthy, her mother is proud, the town admires her, and Jim wants to get engaged, and she postpones her return for Nancy's wedding."
Explain: "That shows how easy it would be to say yes to Ireland, but she has also stopped writing to Tony, who is her husband, and nobody at home knows. So she is living two lives at once."
Link: "This is why the moment when Miss Kelly reveals the secret matters: it ends the situation where she can be in both places."

Typical problems
  • Retelling: three sentences of plot, no explanation. Say: "Pick one moment and tell me what it shows."
  • No Explain step: she names a moment and stops. Ask "So what does that show about her?"
  • Only one home: she talks only about Ireland or only Brooklyn. Ask "Where is the other home in this?"

Screen 11: Question lab (9 min), then Before Tuesday

Choose three or four of the five with the buttons (Character, Theme, Technique, Compare, Longer answer), depending on time. She says a short answer aloud (three or four sentences) and you listen for the four moves. Then click Where to look (the movements and key moments), and last Show a plan, which fills in Point, Evidence, Explain and Link with real moments. The same plans are in the Study Notes. On the Before Tuesday screen, four buttons open the Study Notes in a new tab (six movements, practice questions, building an answer, day-by-day plan): show her where they are.

Answer outlines for all twenty practice questions

These match the numbered questions on the Study Notes page. They are outlines, not scripts. A strong answer picks two or three of the points and explains them.

1. Why does Eilis go to America, and who makes it happen?
Rose and Father Flood arrange it; little work or future for Eilis in Enniscorthy; Eilis goes along rather than choosing; brothers pay passage, Rose handles paperwork. Strong: notes that Eilis does not really choose.
2. Describe the journey and the first days.
Storm, seasickness, Georgina's help and immigration advice; Father Flood meets her; Mrs. Kehoe's boarding house; first work at Bartocci's; homesick; letters leave things out.
3. Who is Tony?
Italian-American plumber; parish dance, walks her home; open, patient, loves baseball; says he loves her; dinner with family; Coney Island; Dodgers; Long Island houses. Strong: shows he is the first to choose her for himself.
4. After Rose's death?
Father Flood tells her at Bartocci's; mother phones; Rose's hidden heart condition; Jack's letter; Eilis turns to Tony; courthouse marriage, told to no one; month at home.
5. The ending.
Rose's room; secret kept; Nancy's wedding; Jim; Miss Kelly knows; Eilis tells her mother, books passage; note at Jim's door; train; imagined smile. Cause chain: secret exposed, so silence ends.
6. Rose.
Arranges the plan; sacrifices her own chance; hides her illness; her death changes Eilis's duty and her closeness to Tony. Theme: sacrifice and silence.
7. Eilis's change.
Start: quiet, carried along, in Rose's shadow. Middle: work, exams, Tony, but silence in letters. End: acts (tells her mother, books passage, note). Qualify: the book ends before we see the result.
8. Father Flood.
Helpful in practice (job, home, tuition, introductions) but not neutral: he makes the case for America, and he is part of the parish that benefits. 'To what extent' answer: trusted as a helper, not as a disinterested adviser.
9. Tony and Jim.
Tony: new life, open feelings, family that is not fully welcoming (Frank). Jim: Enniscorthy, approved by her mother and the town, attentive and shy. Both want to marry her; Tony has a prior claim (secret marriage). They stand for two homes.
10. 'Eilis never really chooses.'
For: Rose and Father Flood arrange it; she drifts toward Jim; says nothing. Against: Tony (she says yes, marries); she acts at the end. Verdict: mostly carried along until forced, then chooses; Miss Kelly is what forces the decision.
11. Home.
Enniscorthy (family, familiarity, reputation) and Brooklyn (work, Tony, independence). Homesickness; Brooklyn like a dream at home; Frank's remark; return. Home as place and person; not settled in the book.
12. Secrets and silence.
Rose's illness; letters that leave out; Tony not told to mother; the marriage; Miss Kelly. Silence protects and lets Eilis avoid choosing; it ends when exposed.
13. Duty.
Rose's sacrifice; Eilis's duty to her mother after Rose dies (Jack's letter); the town and mother approving Jim; she still goes back.
14. Bartocci's.
Welcoming Black customers framed as business; the store goes still, staff stare and pretend not to; customers never come alone; Miss Fortini does not look away. Adds: outsiders and belonging in Brooklyn; looking and not looking; the book's quiet treatment of discomfort.
15. Letters.
Eilis edits for each reader (hides hardship from home; leaves out plumber to Rose; tells her mother nothing about Tony). Shows homesickness and silence; she shapes what each reader knows.
16. Open ending.
Stops at the moment of choosing; image not explanation; reader decides; fits the restrained style; Eilis's smile or Jim's imagined smile.
17. Strong feeling, little description.
Rose's death told simply; the wedding at the courthouse; the note and the train. Effect: reader supplies feeling; restraint.
18. 'Brooklyn is a story about growing up.'
Bildungsroman: from carried along to acting; qualifications, work, love, grief; but the ending is open. Agree, with qualification.
19. Place or person?
Place: Enniscorthy and its reputation; Brooklyn and Bartocci's. Person: Rose, her mother, Tony, Jim. Suggested view: both, and Eilis has to decide which matters more.
20. Final decision freely?
Miss Kelly forces it; pressure from mother and town; but she tells her mother, books passage, leaves a note. Mostly free in the act, shaped by earlier choices and exposure. Open ending.
Worked example (Q20) at the level to aim for

"Eilis makes her final decision mostly freely, but only after she has been forced to. Until the end, she is carried along by other people's plans: Rose and Father Flood send her to America, and at home her mother's pride and the town's approval make a life with Jim look easy. It is Miss Kelly's discovery of the marriage that ends this drifting, because the secret can no longer be kept. After that, Eilis acts for herself: she tells her mother, books her passage, writes to Father Flood, Mrs. Kehoe, and Miss Fortini, and leaves a note for Jim. This shows that her choice is real, even if it was triggered by exposure. The open ending leaves us unsure what she will do next, which suggests that choosing is a process, not a single moment."

A weak version of the same answer, and the fix

Weak: "Yes, she decides to go back to Brooklyn because she loves Tony and she doesn't want to stay in Ireland. She tells everybody and she leaves." (Opinion with no moments, no explanation, and it ignores the pressure to stay.)
Fix by asking: "Which moment shows the pressure to stay? What is the last thing she does before leaving? What made her tell everyone?"

Part 2

Run sheet: the dossier workshop

The rule for this part

She builds the central question and picks the documents. You ask, she says. If she has nothing, give her a frame (an opener) or ask what each document is about in one sentence. Do not hand her a finished problématique.

Screen: The assignment (3 min)

Read the cover page with her. Make sure she knows the row names and what goes in each. The rows come from the official form: Œuvre(s) intégrale(s), Texte(s) littéraire(s), Œuvre(s) d'art visuel, Texte(s) non littéraire(s). Her teacher decides how many documents each row needs and what counts where.

Word choice: the school uses "dossier" (and "portfolio" is the teacher's word too). Use "dossier" with her.

Screens: Full work, literary texts, visual art, non-literary texts (7 min)

Cards appear on screen for each row. Each card says what the document is. Themes and what to know opens the class question, the themes, what she needs to know and the link to Brooklyn; ask her to tell you these before you open it. Add to cover page puts the document on the page on the right, and she can click again to remove it. On the Triple Self-Portrait card there is also a button that opens last week's notes in a new tab. Notice which she picks first. If she picks only literary texts, ask about a visual one, since the dossier should mix types. If she has not studied Maier and Hockney well, let her drop them.

What the Digipad shows her class studied (pages read)
  • Literary texts: Jane Eyre, Chapter IV (Brontë, 1847); "Eveline" from Dubliners (Joyce, 1914). Each ends with a link to Eilis in Brooklyn.
  • Feelings and social expectations: a Wonder Woman comic panel; the lyrics of "Boys Don't Cry" (credited in her textbook to Dempsey, Tolhurst, and Smith, 1979, the Cure); Sigourney Weaver on Ellen Ripley (AFI, 2009). The class debated them in groups.
  • Initiation: Billy Elliot (film) and the Bildungsroman; Maya Angelou, "Still I Rise".
  • Mise en scène de soi: Frida Kahlo (groups presented different self-portraits), Cindy Sherman (each group a different Untitled Film Still), Vivian Maier and David Hockney (textbook, "Disclosing the creative process"), George Orwell, "Why I Write".
  • Personal documents: each student presented one. Several paintings, songs, and films appear, including two Rockwell paintings from classmates. Justine's personal document is the Triple Self-Portrait.

Screen: What is a problématique (4 min)

Do the four ticks and the weak-or-strong warm-up on the problématique screen (the three examples use her own documents). On the connections screen, open each lens and ask which of her chosen documents says the most. On the "Good questions" screen, open one or two of the five examples: ask her how each document would answer it before you open the list. They show what a good question looks like; her own question must be hers, not one of the five unchanged. On the "Your question" screen, she says a question and you type it, and it appears on the cover. If it is a topic, ask "what is the question inside that?". If it is yes-or-no, ask "what could someone say against that?". Test it: can each document answer in one sentence, from a different angle?

More sample problématiques (for your calibration; the five on the Good questions screen are the ones she sees)
  • To what extent do we choose who we become, and to what extent do others choose for us? (Brooklyn, Jane Eyre, Eveline, Kahlo)
  • Is expressing ourselves an act of freedom or a performance? (Jane Eyre, Kahlo, Sherman, Rockwell)
  • How far do we have to leave our past behind in order to build ourselves? (Eveline, Brooklyn, Jane Eyre, Kahlo)
  • Can we ever show who we really are, or do we always stage ourselves? (Sherman, Rockwell, Kahlo, Eilis's letters)

All four fit her documents. Watch that her question is not so broad ("What is identity?") that anything fits, or so narrow that only one document works.

Screen: Connections (4 min)

Open one lens at a time (the screen shows only her chosen documents) and ask: "Which of your documents says the most here?" The lenses are help for finding a question, not a script. If she finds one lens that works with three of her documents, that is her question's angle.

Screens: Your question, then Complete (6 min)

On the last screen read the finished cover with her, row by row. Click any text on the page to fix a title or author (for example, the number of Sherman's still). Then ask her to say the opening sentence of her presentation with her own question and documents. Listen for: the theme named, the question stated clearly, the documents named with author or source. Click Print to save the page as a PDF, or Copy as text, and send it to her.

Likely questions from her teacher, and what a good answer does
  • Why this question? Gives a personal or intellectual reason in one sentence, then names the documents.
  • Which document is the most important? Chooses one and says why in terms of the question.
  • Where do two of your documents disagree? Names a real difference (for example, Jane speaks out, Eveline stays silent) and says what it shows.
  • What does Brooklyn add? Gives a short, specific link (Eilis's letters, her choice at the end), not a plot summary.
  • Why your personal document? Gives her own reason, with two or three details from the picture.

Language to listen for in French speakers

  • "Actually" for "en fait" is a false friend; it means "in fact".
  • "Assist" for "to attend". "Sensible" for "sensitive".
  • "Explain us": she must say "show us" or "explain to us".
  • "The people is" (it is plural). "Since two years" (it is "for").
  • Tense: past tense for the plot ("Eilis went") when the book is in the present ("Eilis goes").
  • Missing links: a string of "and" instead of "because", "although", "so that".
  • Pronunciation worth fixing: "Eilis" (AY-lish), "Tóibín" (toh-BEEN), "Enniscorthy" (en-iss-KOR-thee).

After the lesson

  • Send her the Notes link again and remind her of the plan: Sunday retell the six movements, Monday write three answers and one longer one, Tuesday morning a quick read.
  • Ask her to tell you her central question by Wednesday so you can react to it before Thursday.
  • Find out how the Tuesday exam looked (format, questions) and what the teacher said on Thursday. Add it to the plan for the next session.
