What Happens Between Week 5 and Week 6

Week 5 ended inside the Bartocci's extract: the store starts welcoming Black customers, Eilis is one of the assistants who serves them, and the store goes still every time one comes in. From there, the published summaries I cross-checked agree on this order of events.

Father Flood starts holding dances at the parish hall, and Eilis goes along with other women from Mrs. Kehoe's. At one of them, a young man named Tony asks her to dance and stays with her most of the night. He tells her he's Italian, walks her home, and asks to see her again. They start going out — dinners, dances, movies — and Tony walks her home from her evening bookkeeping classes. Eilis writes to Rose about Tony, but leaves out that he's a plumber, and she doesn't tell her mother about him at all. Father Flood meets Tony, and the two of them hit it off over baseball.

Tony tells Eilis he loves her and talks about having children someday; Eilis isn't ready for that. She passes her first-year exams, and Father Flood tells her a parishioner has already covered her next year of tuition. Tony brings her to dinner with his family — his parents and brothers, in a small, crowded apartment. His youngest brother, Frank, says the family doesn't like Irish people, and later tells Eilis that Tony's last girlfriend got sick on a Coney Island ride. Eilis then gets ready for a day at Coney Island: a bathing suit (Miss Fortini helps at Bartocci's), a hat, and sunglasses. Her reading stops on the Sunday morning when Tony arrives and seems surprised by the sunglasses.

Check these against her book — the summaries disagree or are thin
  • Whether Eilis says "I love you" back before the beach. The summaries conflict on this, so the lesson only says she isn't ready for the children conversation. If Justine says she does say it, or doesn't, trust her book.
  • Who gets called away to a plumbing job at the family dinner. One summary says Tony, another says his father. The lesson avoids the detail.
  • Page numbers. The study guides use different editions, and their page numbers do not line up with her Belin pages (her nylon sale was her teacher's page 153; her beach stop is page 217). Use only the page numbers Justine gives you.
  • A classroom detail from one summary only: the teacher of her evening class, Mr. Rosenblum, hands her a reading list, and Tony reacts oddly when she mentions him. Only one source mentions it, so it is not in the lesson or Notes. If she brings it up, great; if not, leave it.
Handle with care

The bathing suit fitting at Bartocci's: the summaries describe Miss Fortini as pushy about seeing Eilis undressed, which makes it an uncomfortable scene. The lesson mentions only that Miss Fortini helps with the swimsuit and does not offer the fitting as a passage option. If Justine chooses it herself, take it seriously and ask what it shows, rather than steering her away or toward it.

Don't spoil what's next

Anything after the sunglasses is beyond her reading: what happens at the beach itself, the Dodgers game with Tony's brothers, and everything after that. The lesson stops at the morning Tony arrives. If she asks what happens at the beach, say you'd rather she tell you after she's read it.

Stop 2 — Since the Sale

Task

A direct rehearsal of her second task: naming the most important events since the nylon sale. Let her try it cold first, then compare with the check box.

What a strong answer looks like

Five or six events, in the right order, told as a story rather than a list. The essentials: the dances start and she meets Tony; they begin going out and she writes to Rose but not her mother; he tells her he loves her; she passes her exams; the family dinner with Frank's comment; and the preparations for Coney Island. If she lists facts flatly, push her to connect them: "so what changes for Eilis because of that?"

Stop 3 — Your Passage

Task

This stop rehearses her third task. She chooses from six scenes or picks her own, then talks through the two-minute shape out loud. The first four steps of the shape mirror what her teacher asked for last time (story reminder, passage, why it matters, why she chose it), plus a closing personal sentence.

Quick read on each scene
  • The dance: a big change begins in an ordinary place. Good angles: belonging, how Brooklyn starts to feel like hers, chance versus choice.
  • The letter to Rose: what Eilis shares and with whom; ties directly to her silence about Rose's sacrifice and her homesick letters home.
  • "I love you": Eilis holds back. Good angles: readiness, pace, not being swept along.
  • Dinner with Tony's family: belonging and not belonging; Frank's bluntness versus Tony's warmth.
  • The exam results: she is quietly building an independent life; Father Flood's support again, which is worth comparing with how his offer looked back in Week 3.
  • The sunglasses: a small, specific detail that shows Eilis trying something new. Good for a talk about how Tóibín uses small details.
What a strong talk sounds like

It names where the passage sits, says what happens in it without retelling every sentence, links it to one big idea (belonging, family, choice, or the unsaid), and ends with something honestly personal. The weakest version is a plot retelling with no reason for the choice; ask "why this one and not another?" to pull a reason out of her.

Stop 4 & Stop 5

Stop 4 is personal reaction only. Every question is open, so there is no answer key. If she gets stuck, these are possible directions, not answers:

  • The plumber detail: she may expect Rose or their mother to judge Tony's job or class; or she may just be keeping something for herself.
  • What makes her comfortable with Tony: he is patient and open about his feelings, and he doesn't pressure her.
  • Frank's comment: it shows the family's wariness toward outsiders, and the contrast with how Tony treats her.
  • Rose versus her mother: Rose has been her closest confidante all along; her mother is far away, and Eilis has been leaving hard things out of her letters home since the beginning.
  • Ordinary progress alongside the romance: her classes and exams show her building a life of her own, not just falling for someone.

Stop 5 is a pure language drill with example answers built into the reveal button. Just make sure she says full sentences out loud rather than reading the technique label.

Stop 6 — Argue It

Task

The coin decides her side on: "Is Eilis really choosing Tony — or is she choosing the easiest way to stop feeling homesick?" This connects back to the very first theme in her Notes, "Choosing vs. Being Chosen For" — worth pointing out to her if she doesn't make the link herself. Neither side is the "correct" one.

FOR — she's genuinely choosing Tony

  • She keeps saying yes in small, deliberate ways: more dates, walks home after class, and agreeing to meet his family.
  • She holds back when he talks about love and children. Someone merely being swept along wouldn't set her own pace.
  • Writing to Rose about him is an active choice, a trusted person she lets in.
  • Her life is growing in other directions too — classes, exams, her own plans — so Tony is not her only way out of feeling lost.

AGAINST — she's mostly choosing comfort

  • A pattern from the first weeks: Eilis goes along with plans other people make (Rose's, Father Flood's) rather than saying no or yes. Tony could be one more example.
  • She still hides him from her mother, which suggests she hasn't fully decided what he means.
  • When he talks about love and children, her instinct is to hold back, not to answer. Is she choosing him, or just not stopping him?
  • One reading-group guide says that at first she is more interested in Tony as an escape from her housemates than for himself.
