Week 3 — Tutor Guide

Everything you need to run the session without having read the book yourself.

Who's Who (the people this week's stops depend on)

Eilis Lacey — the main character. Quiet, doesn't push for things, doesn't have much going for her at home.

Rose Lacey — Eilis's older sister. Confident, has a real job, well-liked. Important for this week specifically: Rose is the one who actually sets the whole plan to America in motion, working behind the scenes with Father Flood — Eilis doesn't fully realize this until later. That detail matters for Stop 6.

Father Flood — an Irish-American priest, based in a Brooklyn parish, who knows the family through Rose. He's the one who makes the actual offer to Eilis's face, but he's executing a plan Rose already set up, not acting on his own initiative from scratch.

Stop 2 — The Offer

Task

Justine lists what Father Flood actually offers Eilis and the arguments he uses to make it sound worth taking.

What he actually offers — verified
  • A sales job at a department store (Bartocci & Company) in Brooklyn — it pays well, and there's a real path to being promoted into the bookkeeping office later if she finishes her coursework.
  • Official sponsorship — the paperwork needed for her to legally emigrate and be hired.
  • A room already arranged at a boarding house run by an Irish landlady, alongside other young Irish women — she's not arriving with nowhere to live.
  • Ongoing personal follow-up once she's there — when she's overwhelmed with homesickness after arriving, he checks in on her directly and arranges night classes in bookkeeping to help her build toward something better and feel less alone.
Why it sounds so convincing — the contrast

Back home, the best she can find is part-time work at a small grocery store — real bookkeeping jobs are scarce in her town. Father Flood's offer isn't just "a job in America," it's specifically the kind of opportunity she can't get at home, which is exactly why it's hard for her to say no to.

What a strong answer looks like

Names at least three concrete things (job, housing, sponsorship, or the follow-up support) — not just "he gets her a job" — and connects it to why it's specifically better than what's available to her at home.

If she gives a thin answer

Prompt with: "What job, specifically? Where's she going to live? What happens if she's not okay once she gets there?" — the lesson's own reveal box (Check Your List) has this same list if you want to read it together after she's tried.

Stop 3 — Two Sides of the Offer

Task

She holds Father Flood's case (Stop 2) and Eilis's feelings (from Week 2 — ambivalent, reluctant, resigned, torn) at the same time, and uses both to answer whether the offer is really as simple as it sounds.

There is no single correct answer here

This stop is testing a skill (using two sources together), not a fact. Don't correct her toward one "right" conclusion — the strongest answers hold both things as true at once: the opportunity is genuinely good and it still costs her something real. If she only argues one side, that's the moment to nudge her toward the other.

Stop 4 — Unscripted Follow-Up

Task

Five drawn questions, personal-reaction style (e.g. "Have you ever had a big decision made to sound easier than it really was?"). Genuinely no answer key — these are about her own reaction, not the book. If she gives a one-word answer, just ask "why" once.

Stop 5 — Range Practice

Task

Pure language drill — rewording, comparing, reordering, register shifts. Not book-dependent, and the lesson already reveals a model example on click for every round. Nothing extra needed from you here.

Stop 6 — Argue It

Task

Coin flip decides her side on: "Is Father Flood really acting in Eilis's best interest — or is something else going on?" You'll need to help her build a case either way, since she can't pick her side.

FOR — his offer is genuine

  • He doesn't just make the offer and disappear — he personally checks on her when she's struggling, after she's already arrived.
  • The night classes benefit her specifically, long-term, not him.
  • Irish priests helping young emigrants get settled was a real, common, good-faith practice at the time — he's not doing anything unusual or self-serving by the standards of his role.
  • The opportunity is objectively better than what's available to her at home — that part isn't spin.

AGAINST — something else going on

  • He's not the one who came up with this — Rose set the plan in motion, and he's carrying it out. He's presenting someone else's decision as his own offer.
  • Nobody actually asks Eilis what she wants — the "offer" arrives already arranged with her family, more like an announcement than a real choice.
  • A priest helping a family in his old community reflects well on him too — good intentions and a personal or social benefit to him aren't mutually exclusive, and the book doesn't fully rule that out.
  • Whatever his intentions, the practical result is that Eilis is being moved across the Atlantic with very little of her own voice in the decision.

Plot details cross-checked against multiple published study guides and summaries of Brooklyn (Colm Tóibín, 2009) — not the primary text itself. If anything here conflicts with what Justine's class or the book itself says, trust the book.
