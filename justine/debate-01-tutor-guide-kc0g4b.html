Debate 1 — Tutor Guide

Student protests in France, fall 2026 · facts checked October 24, 2026 · Back to the Instructor Hub

How to Run It (60 minutes)

  • Stop 1 · Open (5 min). Two draws, one minute each. No corrections yet; just keep Justine talking.
  • Stop 2 · What's Happening (10 min). Justine answers the four questions before opening the check. Then "Whose Numbers?": the point is that numbers are arguments too.
  • Stop 3 · Every Angle (10 min). At least four groups, including one Justine disagrees with. Push for "they would say..." rather than "they are wrong."
  • Stop 4 · The Thinkers (10 min). Four thinkers, then two Big Questions.
  • Stop 5 · The Method (10 min). All six moves. This is the heart of the lesson.
  • Stop 6 · Argue It (15 min). Two motions at least: one two-minute speech, then switch sides on the same motion.

The Facts Behind Stop 2

What the sources agree on
  • The movement began in late September 2026 at Lycée Antoine-de-Saint-Exupéry in Créteil and spread nationwide; university students joined on October 1.
  • Grievances: teacher and staff shortages, overcrowded classes, dilapidated buildings, heavy timetables, classrooms of 35–40 °C during the heatwaves, and Parcoursup stress. Time reports a ministry finding that nearly 10% of teaching hours in public secondary schools went untaught in 2024–2025.
  • Government: Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu; Education Minister Édouard Geffray; Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez. Lecornu's letter of October 4 set five work streams (classes covered when teachers are absent, building conditions, the school day, clearer orientation and Parcoursup, student involvement), with first decisions promised before the end of October.
  • Geffray's announcements (ministry page, October 8): more than 3,000 replacement teachers, several hundred extra hires, overtime hours for short-term replacements, a reform of lycée student representation (more seats, one third for vocational-track students, budgets for student projects), a "temps lycéen" half-day for student democracy in every lycée from October 7, and an online consultation open until October 23.
  • More than 6,000 arrests, the large majority of them minors (sources give 85–87%). Serious injuries to teenagers were reported, including a 15-year-old who lost a hand to a grenade. Amnesty International called the force disproportionate; Human Rights Watch reported serious injuries to children; the UN Human Rights Office called for de-escalation. Nuñez said the response was proportionate in almost all cases. The police watchdog opened investigations.
  • October 17: wider cost-of-living protests with unions and Yellow Vests, with fires and looting in several cities. The fall break runs October 17 to November 2; students say they will restart when classes resume.
Where the sources disagree — kept off the student page
  • Start date: one source gives September 18, another September 21 for the first protest in Créteil. The lesson says "late September."
  • Turnout: October 6, unions 450,000 vs government 250,000. October 13, ministry 161,500 vs youth unions 480,000. This is used on purpose in "Whose Numbers?"
  • Police injuries: totals range from 600+ to 761, and one report gives about 1,000 for October 17 alone. The lesson says only "hundreds."
  • Arrests: 6,000+ in most reports; Euronews gives 6,877. The lesson says "more than 6,000."
  • Who is behind it: intelligence services reportedly called it organized by La France Insoumise and the far left; LFI calls it spontaneous. The lesson presents both as claims.
  • Anything after about October 23 is not covered. If Justine knows newer developments, let her bring them in and treat them as fresh evidence.

Every Angle — What to Listen For

The skill here is steelmanning: stating a view so fairly that its own supporters would accept it. If Justine caricatures a side ("the government doesn't care"), ask: "What would the minister say if he were here?" The two most useful groups to insist on are the student who just wants to go to class and principals and school staff, because they complicate any easy answer.

The Thinkers — Accuracy Notes

  • Hobbes (Leviathan, 1651): "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" describes life without a common power (the state of nature), not life under a bad government.
  • Rousseau (The Social Contract, 1762): the general will is not simply the majority; it is what serves the common good.
  • Thoreau (Civil Disobedience, 1849): refused a poll tax in protest against slavery and the Mexican–American War; spent one night in jail.
  • Mill (On Liberty, 1859): the harm principle works on both sides of this debate, which is why it is useful.
  • Weber (Politics as a Vocation, 1919): defines the state by its claim to a monopoly on legitimate force. He is describing what a state is, not saying every use of force is right.
  • Camus (L'Homme révolté, 1951): "Je me révolte, donc nous sommes." He defends revolt and warns against revolt that kills or denies others. Justine may know him from school; let her lead.
  • King (Letter from Birmingham Jail, 1963): nonviolent direct action creates "constructive" tension that forces negotiation; whoever breaks an unjust law must do it openly and accept the penalty.
  • Arendt (On Violence, 1970): power and violence are opposites; violence appears where power is being lost.

The Method — What Good Looks Like

This is the French dissertation spoken aloud: définition des termes → problématique → thèse (concede) → antithèse (turn) → références → synthèse. Justine already knows the structure in French; the work is doing it fluently in English, out loud, without notes.

Correct these gently, on the spot
  • "I am agree" → "I agree." · "It depends of" → "It depends on."
  • "Since three weeks" → "for three weeks." · "Make a protest" → "hold / stage a protest."
  • "The youth are..." → "young people are..." (more natural in American English).
  • "Actually" used for actuellement → "currently." "Eventually" for éventuellement → "possibly."
  • "In my opinion, I think" → pick one. Better still: "I would argue that..."

Argue It — Both Sides for Every Motion

Feed these only if Justine runs dry. The goal is two full minutes, all six moves, then the same motion from the other side.

"Blocking your own school is a legitimate way to be heard."

FOR

  • Marches and petitions were easy to ignore; a blockade forces attention.
  • The grievances (untaught hours, heat, broken buildings) were long-standing and documented.
  • Rousseau: students have no vote and little say, so direct action may be their only voice.
  • King: disruptive, nonviolent pressure is what brings authorities to the table.

AGAINST

  • It punishes the students who want to learn, often the ones who can least afford lost hours.
  • Mill: your freedom to protest stops where it blocks someone else's freedom to attend class.
  • Blockades created the conditions for violence, fires and injured staff.
  • Legitimate means more than effective: legal channels (student councils, consultation) exist.

"Violence is never justified, even for a just cause."

FOR

  • Arendt: violence can destroy power but cannot create it; it wrecks the movement's credibility.
  • Camus: a revolt that harms others betrays the very solidarity it started from.
  • The violence gave the government a reason to talk about order instead of schools.
  • Teenagers, staff and police were seriously injured; nothing in the demands requires that.

AGAINST

  • "Never" is a strong word: history has revolts many people now admire.
  • Weber: the state itself uses force; the question is what makes force legitimate, not whether it exists.
  • Distinguish violence against people from damage to property; many philosophers treat them differently.
  • Careful: the student should argue this in the abstract. Steer away from endorsing any specific act of violence.

"At sixteen, you are old enough to have a real political voice."

FOR

  • The students are the ones living with the conditions every day; they are the experts on their own school.
  • Several countries (for example Austria) let sixteen-year-olds vote in national elections.
  • The movement organized itself, held assemblies and drafted demands.
  • The government is now adding student seats to decision-making bodies, which admits the point.

AGAINST

  • Teenagers can be more easily influenced, which is exactly the "manipulation" accusation.
  • Having a voice is not the same as having the final say; adults carry the legal responsibility.
  • Most of those arrested were minors, which shows the risks fall hardest on them.
  • A voice can be heard through councils and consultations without blockades.

"The government was right to close schools to keep order."

FOR

  • Hobbes and Weber: keeping people safe is the state's first job.
  • Schools were set on fire, and principals and staff were injured.
  • Closures with remote classes kept some teaching going while protecting students.
  • Temporary closures are proportionate compared with mass arrests.

AGAINST

  • Closing schools does exactly what the blockades did: students lose class time.
  • It treats a protest about schools as a public-order problem, not an education problem.
  • Rousseau: decisions taken without the people they affect lack legitimacy.
  • It may have pushed students onto the streets, where the risks were higher.

"When teenagers have to protest, the adults have already failed."

FOR

  • The ministry's own figure: nearly 10% of teaching hours went untaught in 2024–2025.
  • Shortages, heat and run-down buildings were known for years.
  • Teachers' unions themselves called the system "in crisis."
  • Young people protesting is a sign that normal channels did not work.

AGAINST

  • Protest is a normal part of democratic life, especially in France; it is not proof of failure.
  • Some problems (heatwaves, recruitment) are hard and take time, not neglect.
  • The adults are now responding: 3,000 replacement teachers, consultation, reform.
  • "The adults" is too vague: which adults — parents, teachers, government, voters?

"Calling a movement "manipulated" is a way of refusing to listen to it."

FOR

  • It moves the discussion from the students' demands to who is behind them.
  • It assumes teenagers can't think for themselves.
  • The grievances are concrete and local, varying from school to school, which fits a spontaneous movement.
  • Experts quoted in the press say students can tell real support from opportunism.

AGAINST

  • Political parties really do try to use movements before an election; pointing that out is fair.
  • A warning about manipulation can protect young people from being used.
  • You can listen to the demands and still criticize those who exploit them.
  • Both things can be true at once: genuine anger and political opportunism.
