Brooklyn — Part One Reference

Teacher reference · covers roughly what Justine has read on her own so far

This is a factual summary written for teaching purposes, in our own words — not quoted text from the novel. Use it to check what Justine's retelling gets right, what she skips, and what's worth pushing on.

Characters

Eilis LaceyYoung woman in her early twenties, the novel's central character. Practical, quiet, more observant than she lets on.
Rose LaceyEilis's older sister. Has a good job, is more socially confident, and is the one who arranges Eilis's move to America.
Mrs. LaceyEilis and Rose's widowed mother. Depends on her daughters; her reaction to Eilis leaving is a quiet source of guilt.
Father FloodAn Irish priest working in Brooklyn who sponsors Eilis's move and arranges her job and lodging in America.
GeorginaAn Irish woman Eilis meets on the ship crossing to America — more experienced, helps Eilis navigate the voyage and arrival.
Mrs. KehoeRuns the Brooklyn boarding house where Eilis lives with several other young Irish women once she arrives.

Plot Beats — Part One

1

Eilis lives a limited life in a small Irish town — few job prospects, overshadowed socially by Rose.

2

Rose and Father Flood arrange for Eilis to emigrate to Brooklyn for better opportunity — a job and a future Ireland can't offer her.

3

Eilis feels torn — she doesn't strongly want to leave, but doesn't strongly object either. The decision is made as much for her as by her.

4

The sea crossing is rough — physically (seasickness) and emotionally. Georgina helps her get through it practically.

5

Arrival in Brooklyn: a boarding house, a shop job, night classes in bookkeeping. Everything is unfamiliar.

6

Severe homesickness sets in — not dramatic, but constant, quiet, and hard to explain to anyone around her.

Themes to Recognize

Leaving vs. being sent Homesickness as something private Duty to family A new life that doesn't feel chosen

What to Check Her Retelling Against

Does she get the "why" right, not just the "what"? It's easy to say "she moved to America." It's harder — and more valuable — to say "her sister arranged it and she didn't strongly resist."

Does she mention how Eilis actually feels, or just what happens to her? The ambivalence (not fully wanting to go, not fully refusing) is the heart of Part One.

Does she connect the voyage to the homesickness that follows? The crossing isn't just a travel scene — it's the hinge between her old life and the isolation of the new one.
