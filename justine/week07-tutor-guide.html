What Happens From the Beach to the End

Week 6 stopped on the morning of the Coney Island trip. These events come from five published sources, and the order is consistent across them. The numbers match the model timeline in Stop 2.

  1. Coney Island. Eilis and Tony go to the beach and stay in the shallow water because Tony is not a strong swimmer. That summer he takes her to a Dodgers game with his brothers (Maurice, Laurence, Frank); she has her first beer and hot dog there.
  2. Tony's plan. Around Christmas, Tony tells her his brothers bought land on Long Island to build five houses, and he hopes one will be for the two of them. It is an indirect proposal. Eilis doesn't answer and writes to Rose for advice.
  3. Rose dies. Father Flood comes to Bartocci's and tells Eilis that Rose died in the night. Her mother calls that evening: Rose had a heart condition and had hidden it from the family. Tony is waiting when she gets home, and they go to Mass in Rose's memory the next day.
  4. Jack's letter. A brother writes that the brothers have to go back to England and Eilis should go home and look after their mother.
  5. The night with Tony. Upset, Eilis goes to Tony's house in the middle of the night. His family wakes up, so they go to a diner, and afterward she is too upset to go inside alone and they spend the night together. Mrs. Kehoe acts strangely and locks the basement door; Father Flood looks at Eilis oddly after Mass. Eilis makes Tony go to confession with her at a church where no one knows them.
  6. Leave. Eilis tells Father Flood she wants to go to Ireland for a month. Her visit makes him kinder, and Mrs. Kehoe removes the lock.
  7. The secret wedding. Before her second-semester exams, she tells Tony she is going home. He asks her to marry him before she leaves, with a simple courthouse ceremony and a public wedding later. They have dinner with his family the Sunday before the wedding and marry soon after her exams, then go to Coney Island, where Eilis looks happily at her ring.
  8. Home. Her mother shows her Rose's untouched room. They write thank-you notes for the funeral. Eilis refuses Rose's clothes, lays a wreath on the grave, tells no one about Tony or the marriage, and writes to Tony that she will stay a few extra days.
  9. Nancy's wedding. Nancy is marrying George Sheridan, and Eilis's mother has already said Eilis will be there. Eilis postpones her return so she can go.
  10. Jim Farrell. At a beach outing arranged by Nancy, Eilis is surprised to find Jim there. He acts as if they are a couple, and she plays along. They swim, go dancing at a hotel, and kiss. Meanwhile, Rose's old office offers her bookkeeping work and then a permanent job. The town admires her, and her mother approves of Jim.
  11. The engagement question. Jim drives Eilis and her mother to the wedding. Afterward, on the beach, he says he wants to get engaged before she leaves. She doesn't answer. She has stopped writing to Tony, and at one point tells herself she no longer loves him.
  12. Miss Kelly. Miss Kelly calls Eilis to her shop and shows that she knows about the marriage, through Mrs. Kehoe (one source says Miss Kelly and Mrs. Kehoe are cousins).
  13. The decision. Eilis buys her passage back, writes to Father Flood, Mrs. Kehoe and Miss Fortini, writes to Tony that she loves him, and tells her mother she is married.
  14. The last morning. She leaves a note at Jim's door promising to explain by letter and takes the train. She imagines her mother telling Jim that she has gone back to Brooklyn, and the book ends with her smiling at how much that phrase will come to mean.
Check these against her book — the sources disagree or are thin
  • Her mother's reaction when Eilis says she is married. One source says she is understanding and says Tony must be a nice man; another says she hides her devastation; another says she seems to know already. The lesson leaves this open. Trust Justine's reading of the scene.
  • The letters. One source says Eilis hides letters from Tony to Rose in Rose's room; another says Eilis suspects her mother found letters while sorting Rose's belongings. Not in the lesson.
  • The beach photograph with Jim. Sources differ on whether Eilis keeps it, leaves it with her note, or destroys it. Not in the lesson.
  • Miss Kelly's tone. One source says she threatens to expose Eilis; the others say she only shows she knows. The lesson says she "lets her know."
  • Father Flood bringing the news of Rose's death in person at Bartocci's. Only one source describes this scene. The others say only that Eilis learns Rose has died and speaks to her mother by phone. It is in the lesson because it is consistent with the rest.
  • Jim's golf tournament in Rose's honor. Single source. Not in the lesson.
  • Mr. Rosenblum, her evening-class teacher. Single source in earlier weeks. Not in the lesson.
  • Page numbers. The study guides use different editions and do not line up with her Belin pages. Use only the pages Justine gives you.
Handle with care

The night after Jack's letter, Eilis and Tony become physically intimate, and the book handles it quietly. The lesson says only that "they become close" and leaves the rest alone. If Justine asks about it or chooses it as a passage, treat it as a normal literary question about restraint and what the book leaves out. Rose's death is also an emotional scene, so let her take her time with it if she picks it.

Stop 1 — Open

Task

Six everyday prompts that warm her up and echo the book's ideas: secrets, home, choice, distance, return. No answer key. A full-sentence answer for a minute or so is a good result.

Stop 2 — Since the Beach

Task

A direct rehearsal of the "tell the story" task that every spoken evaluation includes. Let her try it cold first, then compare with the check box.

Answers to the five prompts
  • The sad news: Rose has died suddenly, in her sleep. Father Flood tells her at Bartocci's, and her mother follows up by phone.
  • What Tony asks: he asks Eilis to marry him before she leaves for Ireland. They marry in a simple courthouse ceremony, in secret, with a public wedding supposed to follow later.
  • What she keeps to herself: that she is married, and that she has a boyfriend in America at all.
  • Jim Farrell: a young man from Enniscorthy whom she met earlier in the book. At home he courts her and the town and her mother approve. They kiss, and he asks about an engagement.
  • What forces the decision: Miss Kelly shows that she knows Eilis is married. Eilis then books her return, tells her mother, and leaves.
What a strong answer looks like

Eight or nine of the twelve beats, in the right order, told as a story with cause and effect: Rose's death leads to Jack's letter, which leads to the trip home, which is why Tony asks her to marry him first. A weak answer is a list with no connections, or one that gets the order of the wedding and the trip wrong.

Stop 3 — The Whole Book

Task

Six cards covering the whole book, plus the Five-Minute Talk shape. The cards are the building blocks; the talk is the real rehearsal. Have her pick two or three cards she could speak on for a minute each, then run the full talk once without notes.

What to listen for on each card
  • Eilis: start to finish: a clear before and after. A good answer says she gains independence and confidence but keeps her habit of staying quiet about what matters to her. Either "she has changed" or "only her situation has" can work if it is backed with examples.
  • Two places: Enniscorthy is small, close and watchful; Brooklyn is crowded and mixed, and it is where she works and studies. Both beach scenes (Coney Island with Tony, the outings with Jim) are good evidence for comparing the two places.
  • Secrets and letters: a pattern across the whole book: what she leaves out of her letters home, what she leaves out about Tony, the secret marriage, and finally the note to Jim. A strong answer sees the pattern, not just one example.
  • Choice or drift?: links back to the first week's theme, "Choosing vs. Being Chosen For." Strong answers use at least one example from the beginning and one from the end.
  • How Tóibín tells it: close third-person narration; plain, restrained language; important events told quietly; small details that carry meaning. This is an interpretation, so accept anything she can support with an example.
  • The ending: the book stops as she leaves Enniscorthy, before any reunion with Tony. Good answers discuss openness and what her smile suggests. One reading-group guide asks the same question: why end before she boards the ship?
What a strong Five-Minute Talk sounds like

It has a clear opening (title, author, setting), tells the story in three moves, makes one argument about a big idea and proves it with a specific moment, zooms in on one passage, notes one thing about Tóibín's style, and ends with a personal sentence. It sounds like a conversation and not like a recited summary. If she runs long, cut the passage section first; if she is too short, ask for one more example.

Genre — confirm with her teacher

Her Week 5 assignment asked her to explain how a passage relates to the genre. Common labels for Brooklyn are a migration novel and a coming-of-age novel (in French, a roman d'apprentissage). That is my interpretation, not something the study guides state, so find out which term her class uses and let her use it.

Stop 4 — Follow-Up

Every question is open, so there is no single answer key. If she gets stuck, these are possible directions, not answers:

  • Different and the same: different: more independent, has a job, a degree path, a husband, and has seen another world. The same: she still avoids hard conversations and lets events come to her until someone forces the issue.
  • Why she doesn't tell her mother: her mother is in mourning and wants her to stay; Eilis is afraid of upsetting her; she has hidden hard things from home since the beginning; and she half-wonders if staying would be easier.
  • Is Jim a real alternative: in his favor, he is attentive, kind to her, has a stable future, and Eilis enjoys his company. Against: the town and her mother want the match, and she cannot say yes without revealing she is married. At one point she even tells herself she no longer loves Tony, which suggests she is being swept along by the town's approval.
  • Why end before Brooklyn: it keeps the focus on her decision and not on the reunion; it leaves the future open; and it echoes the opening, where Eilis's life was arranged by others.
  • Why Rose still matters: her room, her clothes, her grave, her office job; Rose's absence shapes how her mother treats Eilis and what Eilis is offered. She also set the whole plan in motion by sending Eilis to America.

Stop 5 — Range Practice

Pure language drill; example answers are built into the reveal button. Make sure she says full sentences aloud rather than reading the technique label. Any grammatically correct variation that uses the technique counts.

Stop 6 — Argue It

Task

The coin decides her side on: "At the end of the book, does Eilis choose her own life — or do other people and events choose it for her?" This is the same idea as the first theme in her Notes, "Choosing vs. Being Chosen For," now answered across the whole book. Neither side is the "correct" one.

FOR — she chooses her own life

  • She builds her own life in Brooklyn: classes, exams, job, Tony. These are her own efforts.
  • She says yes to Tony and marries him; no one makes her.
  • At home she could have stayed with Jim, a stable job, and her mother. She leaves anyway.
  • She tells her mother the truth, writes the letters, buys the ticket. The final actions are hers.

AGAINST — other people and events choose for her

  • The beginning: Rose and Father Flood arrange the whole move, and Eilis goes along.
  • Rose's death, then Jack's letter, send her home. She did not decide to go.
  • Miss Kelly's revelation is what forces the decision. Without it, Eilis might have kept drifting.
  • She postpones, doesn't answer Jim, and keeps her secrets. Her habit is to avoid choosing until someone corners her.

A good closing question for either side: even if Miss Kelly forces the timing, who decides what Eilis does with it? The ending's smile suggests she takes ownership of the choice, but it is the reader's interpretation.

Likely Evaluation Questions

Whatever format the evaluation takes, these are the kinds of questions it tends to ask. Use them in a second session, or drop one in at the end of this one. For each, a strong answer names a specific moment from the book and says what it shows.

  • Retell and remind: "Introduce the book and remind us of its main elements." Needs: title, author, 1950s, Eilis, Enniscorthy to Brooklyn and back, Rose, Tony, Jim, the ending.
  • Character: "How does Eilis change?" or "What does Rose represent?" Needs: before and after, with examples.
  • Theme: "Home and belonging," "choice," "secrecy," or "family duty." Needs: one example from each part of the book.
  • Passage: "Choose an extract and explain why it matters." Needs: where it sits, what happens, what it shows, why she chose it.
  • Comparison: "Compare Coney Island with the beach scenes in Ireland," or "Compare Tony and Jim." Needs: two or three specific points on each side.
  • Form and style: "Why does Tóibín tell the story this way?" or "Why does it end here?" Needs: one technique, with an example, and an effect.
  • Opinion: "Did Eilis make the right choice?" Needs: a position, two reasons, and acknowledgment of the other side.
