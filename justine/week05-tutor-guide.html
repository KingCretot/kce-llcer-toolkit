What Happens Between Week 4 and Week 5

A lot — more than usual, because her class's real pace (she confirmed they've read to page 94, Belin Education edition) is further along than the next single beat after Week 4. The voyage over is rough: a bad storm, and Eilis is seasick for most of it. She shares a third-class cabin with Georgina, a more seasoned transatlantic traveler, who gets her through the worst of the sickness and coaches her on how to present herself and pass through immigration before they land.

Father Flood meets Eilis in Brooklyn and settles her into a boarding house run by Mrs. Kehoe, an Irish widow, alongside several other young Irish women. She starts working at Bartocci's, a department store, selling women's clothing, trained by a few of the staff there including Miss Fortini. She's homesick, and her letters home leave out the hard parts — the seasickness, how strange everything still feels.

Then the extract Justine's actually been assigned: Bartocci's decides to start selling stockings in new shades made for darker skin tones, explicitly as a way to bring in Black customers — framed by the store as a business decision, first on the street to do it. Eilis is one of the assistants assigned to serve these new customers. The first time it happens she catches herself staring, then notices everyone else in the store is staring too. As the week goes on and more customers come in, the pattern repeats every time: the store goes still, staff look down and stay busy rather than be caught watching, and Miss Fortini is the one person who never looks away from the counter — her reason for that isn't stated in the text, which is deliberate; it's one of the better things for Justine to raise as an open question rather than something she "explains." Miss Delano steps back and lets Eilis serve each time. By the end of the extract: none of these women ever comes in alone, and most don't look at Eilis or speak to her directly. It isn't confined to the store, either — some of Eilis's fellow boarders at Mrs. Kehoe's disapprove of her serving these customers.

Stop 2 — The Story So Far

Task

This is a direct rehearsal of the first thing she's graded on Tuesday — recapping the story from memory. Let her try it cold first, then check against the reveal box.

What a strong answer looks like

Doesn't need to hit every beat in the reveal box — it needs to flow as a spoken narrative, not a list. If she's reciting facts in a flat sequence, push her toward connecting them: "so what does that mean for her once she gets there?" If she skips the voyage or the boarding house entirely, that's fine content-wise but worth a gentle nudge, since "where does she live" is the kind of detail a teacher might follow up on.

Stop 3 — The Nylon Sale

Task

This is the real assigned extract. Treat the reveal box as the content delivery itself if she hasn't finished reading — read it together if needed, out loud, before asking her to answer the prompts.

What actually happens — verified
  • Bartocci's decides to start serving Black customers, tied specifically to new nylon stocking shades for darker skin tones — explicitly a business move, "first on the street" to do it.
  • Eilis is one of the assistants chosen to serve them.
  • She catches herself staring the first time, then notices the whole store is staring.
  • Each time it happens afterward: the store goes still, staff look down and stay busy, nobody wants to be caught watching — except Miss Fortini, who never looks away.
  • By the end: no woman ever comes in alone, and most avoid eye contact or direct speech with Eilis.
  • Some of Eilis's housemates at Mrs. Kehoe's disapprove of her role in this.
Genuinely open — don't push toward one answer

Why Miss Fortini never looks away is never explained in the text. Resist the urge to supply a reason if Justine asks — "why do you think?" is the right response, not an answer key. Same with whether the store's motives matter if the outcome (more respectful service, more customers served) is positive either way — there's a real argument on both sides, which is exactly what Stop 6 is for.

Stop 6 — Argue It

Task

Coin decides her side on: "Is Bartocci's decision to welcome coloured shoppers a genuine step forward — or just smart business dressed up as progress?" This is the same underlying question as Week 3's Father Flood debate, applied to an institution instead of a person — worth pointing that connection out to her directly if she doesn't make it herself.

FOR — it's genuine progress

  • Whatever the store's reasons, the actual effect is that Black customers are now being served somewhere they weren't before — that's a real, material change, not just a gesture.
  • Miss Fortini's own behavior — actually making sure the customers are served properly, never looking away — suggests at least one person on the floor treats this as more than a sales tactic.
  • Businesses often lead on social change before attitudes do, precisely because money moves faster than opinion. That doesn't make the outcome less real.
  • Progress rarely arrives with pure motives attached in the real world either — judging it only by motive risks dismissing every imperfect first step.

AGAINST — it's really about profit

  • The store's own pitch is commercial, not moral — "first on the street to sell" new stocking shades is a competitive sales line, not a statement of principle.
  • None of the discomfort in the store actually goes away — staff still stare, still look down, still avoid the counter. The policy changed; the atmosphere didn't.
  • The customers themselves seem to sense this isn't a comfortable welcome — not one comes in alone, most won't make eye contact. That's not how people act somewhere they feel genuinely wanted.
  • Calling it progress lets the store take credit for something its own staff and customers don't yet experience as real change.
