Brooklyn — Your Notes

From Ireland to Brooklyn — through Tony and the trip to Coney Island

These notes stop exactly where your reading stops — nothing here happens after this point in the book. Use it to check your understanding, not to skip ahead.

Characters

Eilis LaceyThe main character. Young, quiet, and pays close attention to things without saying much about it. She's now making her way through a brand-new life in Brooklyn.
Rose LaceyEilis's older sister, back home in Ireland. She's the one who set the plan to America in motion — and is quietly sacrificing her own chance to leave by doing it.
Mrs. LaceyTheir mother — a widow, still in Ireland. Eilis writes to her, but leaves out most of what's actually hard about this new life.
Father FloodThe Irish priest who arranged Eilis's job and sponsorship. He meets her when she arrives in Brooklyn and gets her settled.
Mrs. KehoeEilis's landlady in Brooklyn — an Irish widow who runs the boarding house where Eilis now lives, alongside several other young Irish women.
Miss FortiniOne of the women who trains Eilis at Bartocci's. During the tensest moments at the counter, she's the one person who never looks away — it's not yet clear exactly why.
TonyA young Italian-American plumber Eilis meets at a parish dance. He's open about how he feels, patient, and loves baseball. He's the first person in Brooklyn who seems to choose Eilis for himself.
FrankTony's youngest brother. Blunt — at dinner, he says out loud that the family doesn't like Irish people, a moment that shows Eilis being welcomed into a family that isn't entirely welcoming.

The Story So Far

1

Eilis lives a small, limited life in her Irish town. There isn't much work for her there, and she's often in Rose's shadow.

2

Rose and Father Flood arrange for Eilis to go to America — a real job and a real opportunity, things she can't easily get at home.

3

Father Flood lays out his case to Eilis directly — a real job waiting, a place to live, someone to turn to if she needs help. It's presented as simple and generous. Whether it actually is that simple is worth questioning.

4

Eilis doesn't fight the plan, but she doesn't really choose it either. She goes along with it.

5

In the weeks before she leaves, her brothers agree to pay for her passage, and Rose handles the paperwork. The night before she goes, Rose helps her pack and gives her jewelry and money.

6

During that night, Eilis realizes Rose is sacrificing her own future to make this happen for her — and says nothing about it.

7

The voyage over is rough — a bad storm, and Eilis is seasick for most of it. She shares a cabin with Georgina, a more experienced traveler, who gets her through the worst of it and helps her prepare to pass through immigration.

8

Father Flood meets her when she arrives in Brooklyn and gets her settled into Mrs. Kehoe's boarding house, alongside several other young Irish women.

9

She starts working at Bartocci's, a department store, trained by a few of the women there, including Miss Fortini. She's homesick, and the letters she writes home leave out how hard it actually is.

10

Bartocci's decides to start welcoming Black customers, tied to new stocking shades made for darker skin tones — explicitly framed as a smart business move.

11

Eilis is one of the assistants chosen to serve these new customers. Every time one comes in, the store goes still and watchful — and not one of them ever comes in alone.

12

Father Flood starts holding dances at the parish hall. Eilis goes along with other women from Mrs. Kehoe's, and at one of them a young man named Tony asks her to dance. He tells her he's Italian, walks her home, and asks to see her again.

13

They start going out — dinners, dances, movies — and Tony walks her home from her evening classes. Eilis writes to Rose about him, but leaves out that he's a plumber, and she doesn't tell her mother at all. Father Flood meets Tony too, and the two of them hit it off over baseball.

14

Tony tells Eilis he loves her and talks about having children someday. Eilis isn't ready for that yet.

15

Eilis passes her first-year exams. Father Flood tells her a parishioner has already paid for her next year of tuition.

16

Tony brings her to dinner with his family — his parents and brothers, in a small, crowded apartment. His youngest brother, Frank, says the family doesn't like Irish people, and later tells her that Tony's last girlfriend got sick on a Coney Island ride.

17

Eilis gets ready for a day at Coney Island with Tony: a bathing suit (Miss Fortini helps at Bartocci's), a hat, and sunglasses. On Sunday morning Tony arrives and seems surprised by the sunglasses. That's where your reading stops.

Themes to Think About

Choosing vs. Being Chosen For

Nobody forces Eilis to go. But nobody really asks her either. She just... doesn't say no.

Is going along with something the same as choosing it?
Duty to Family

Rose is doing this because she loves Eilis and wants better for her. Eilis, in turn, doesn't want to disappoint Rose or her mother by refusing.

Can loving someone be a reason you stop listening to what you actually want?
Feeling Sad Before Anything Has Even Happened

Eilis hasn't left yet — but there's already a heaviness in how she's living these last days at home.

Can you grieve something before you've even lost it?
Persuasion and Motive

Father Flood's offer sounds generous. But he's not a neutral party making it — he's also the one making the case.

Can an offer still be genuine if the person making it also gains something when you accept it?
Silence and Sacrifice

Rose is giving something up too, quietly. So is Eilis, by not saying what she actually feels. Nobody in this scene says out loud what they're really going through.

Is staying silent to protect someone else its own kind of sacrifice — or just another way of not choosing?
Watching Without Looking

When the new customers come in, almost nobody actually looks at them directly — but everyone is watching. The staff stare and pretend not to; the customers avoid eye contact too, and never come in alone.

Is looking away a form of politeness, or just another way of not dealing with something uncomfortable?
Telling and Not Telling

Eilis writes to Rose about Tony — but leaves out that he's a plumber, and she doesn't tell her mother about him at all. She's still deciding, letter by letter, what each person gets to know.

Is leaving something out the same as lying — or is it something else?
Belonging

In Brooklyn, Eilis starts building something of her own: classes, exams, a boyfriend. But Frank's comment at dinner shows that belonging to one person doesn't mean everyone around them welcomes you.

What does it take to belong somewhere — a place, a person, or both?

The Conflict Right Now

None of this is resolved yet — and that's the point. Eilis left Ireland still caught between the safe, familiar life she knew and an opportunity she didn't fully ask for. She's since made the crossing and started her new life in Brooklyn — but leaving didn't actually settle anything. The tension just traveled with her.

Father Flood's offer is still worth weighing on its own terms: he wasn't a neutral party making it, and he's not around this week to answer for it — but the question of whether a generous-looking offer can still be genuine hasn't gone anywhere.

Neither has Rose's sacrifice, or Eilis's silence about it. She still hasn't said a word about any of it — not to Rose, and not in her letters home either.

There's a new layer now, too: Bartocci's decision to serve Black customers is framed as generous, but it's also clearly good business — and once it starts happening, almost nobody in the store will look directly at anyone else. Whether that's politeness, discomfort, or something closer to fear is left for you to decide.

And there's a different kind of tension now. Tony is open about how he feels. Eilis is still deciding how much to say — to Tony, to Rose, and to her mother, who knows nothing about him. Frank's story about the last girl Tony took to Coney Island hangs over the day she's about to have.

Your reading now ends on the morning of that trip. Whether Eilis is truly choosing Tony, or choosing the easiest way to stop feeling homesick, is left for you to decide.
