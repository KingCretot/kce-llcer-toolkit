Brooklyn — Your Notes

From Ireland to Brooklyn and back again — the whole novel

These notes now cover the whole novel, including the ending. Finish the book first, then use them to check your understanding and to prepare for your evaluation.

Characters

Eilis LaceyThe main character. Young, quiet, and pays close attention to things without saying much about it. She's now making her way through a brand-new life in Brooklyn.
Rose LaceyEilis's older sister, back home in Ireland. She's the one who set the plan to America in motion — and is quietly sacrificing her own chance to leave by doing it.
Mrs. LaceyTheir mother — a widow, still in Ireland. Eilis writes to her, but leaves out most of what's actually hard about this new life.
Father FloodThe Irish priest who arranged Eilis's job and sponsorship. He meets her when she arrives in Brooklyn and gets her settled.
Mrs. KehoeEilis's landlady in Brooklyn — an Irish widow who runs the boarding house where Eilis now lives, alongside several other young Irish women.
Miss FortiniOne of the women who trains Eilis at Bartocci's. During the tensest moments at the counter, she's the one person who never looks away — it's not yet clear exactly why.
TonyA young Italian-American plumber Eilis meets at a parish dance. He's open about how he feels, patient, and loves baseball. He's the first person in Brooklyn who seems to choose Eilis for himself.
FrankTony's youngest brother. Blunt — at dinner, he says out loud that the family doesn't like Irish people, a moment that shows Eilis being welcomed into a family that isn't entirely welcoming.
JackOne of Eilis's brothers, who lives in England. After Rose dies, he writes that Eilis should come home and look after their mother.
NancyEilis's friend in Enniscorthy. Her wedding to George Sheridan is one reason Eilis stays longer than planned, and she helps bring Eilis and Jim together.
Jim FarrellA young man from Enniscorthy. Attentive and a little shy, he courts Eilis once she is home. He represents a comfortable, approved future in Ireland, and he asks about an engagement.
Miss KellyThe shopkeeper in Enniscorthy, whom Eilis worked for before she left. Her knowledge of Eilis's secret marriage forces Eilis to decide.

The Story So Far

1

Eilis lives a small, limited life in her Irish town. There isn't much work for her there, and she's often in Rose's shadow.

2

Rose and Father Flood arrange for Eilis to go to America — a real job and a real opportunity, things she can't easily get at home.

3

Father Flood lays out his case to Eilis directly — a real job waiting, a place to live, someone to turn to if she needs help. It's presented as simple and generous. Whether it actually is that simple is worth questioning.

4

Eilis doesn't fight the plan, but she doesn't really choose it either. She goes along with it.

5

In the weeks before she leaves, her brothers agree to pay for her passage, and Rose handles the paperwork. The night before she goes, Rose helps her pack and gives her jewelry and money.

6

During that night, Eilis realizes Rose is sacrificing her own future to make this happen for her — and says nothing about it.

7

The voyage over is rough — a bad storm, and Eilis is seasick for most of it. She shares a cabin with Georgina, a more experienced traveler, who gets her through the worst of it and helps her prepare to pass through immigration.

8

Father Flood meets her when she arrives in Brooklyn and gets her settled into Mrs. Kehoe's boarding house, alongside several other young Irish women.

9

She starts working at Bartocci's, a department store, trained by a few of the women there, including Miss Fortini. She's homesick, and the letters she writes home leave out how hard it actually is.

10

Bartocci's decides to start welcoming Black customers, tied to new stocking shades made for darker skin tones — explicitly framed as a smart business move.

11

Eilis is one of the assistants chosen to serve these new customers. Every time one comes in, the store goes still and watchful — and not one of them ever comes in alone.

12

Father Flood starts holding dances at the parish hall. Eilis goes along with other women from Mrs. Kehoe's, and at one of them a young man named Tony asks her to dance. He tells her he's Italian, walks her home, and asks to see her again.

13

They start going out — dinners, dances, movies — and Tony walks her home from her evening classes. Eilis writes to Rose about him, but leaves out that he's a plumber, and she doesn't tell her mother at all. Father Flood meets Tony too, and the two of them hit it off over baseball.

14

Tony tells Eilis he loves her and talks about having children someday. Eilis isn't ready for that yet.

15

Eilis passes her first-year exams. Father Flood tells her a parishioner has already paid for her next year of tuition.

16

Tony brings her to dinner with his family — his parents and brothers, in a small, crowded apartment. His youngest brother, Frank, says the family doesn't like Irish people, and later tells her that Tony's last girlfriend got sick on a Coney Island ride.

17

Eilis gets ready for a day at Coney Island with Tony: a bathing suit (Miss Fortini helps at Bartocci's), a hat, and sunglasses. On Sunday morning Tony arrives and seems surprised by the sunglasses. The next beats pick up from there.

18

Coney Island. Eilis and Tony spend the day in the shallow water, because Tony isn't a strong swimmer. Later that summer he takes her to a Dodgers game with his brothers.

19

Around Christmas, Tony explains that his brothers are building houses on Long Island and hopes one will be for him and Eilis. It's almost a proposal. Eilis doesn't answer directly, and she writes to Rose for advice.

20

Rose dies suddenly in the night. Father Flood tells Eilis at Bartocci's, and her mother phones that evening and explains that Rose had a heart condition she had hidden from everyone.

21

Jack writes that Eilis should come home to look after their mother. Overwhelmed, she turns to Tony, and they become close. Mrs. Kehoe and Father Flood seem to guess. Eilis asks Father Flood to help her get a month away to visit home.

22

Before she goes, Tony asks her to marry him. They have a simple courthouse wedding and tell no one, with a public ceremony planned for later.

23

Back in Enniscorthy, Eilis finds Rose's room untouched. She writes thank-you notes with her mother and visits the grave. She tells nobody about Tony or the marriage.

24

Nancy's wedding to George Sheridan is coming up, and Eilis's mother has already said Eilis will attend, so Eilis postpones her return.

25

Eilis meets Jim Farrell again at a beach outing, and a romance begins: swimming, dancing, a kiss. She is also offered work at the office where Rose worked. The town admires her, and her mother approves of Jim.

26

After the wedding, Jim says he wants to get engaged before she goes back to America. Eilis doesn't answer. She has stopped writing to Tony.

27

Miss Kelly calls Eilis to her shop and shows she has heard about the marriage from Mrs. Kehoe. The secret is out.

28

Eilis books her passage, writes to Father Flood, Mrs. Kehoe, and Miss Fortini, and tells her mother she is married to Tony.

29

On the last morning she leaves a note at Jim's door promising to explain, and takes the train. The book ends with her imagining Jim hearing that she has gone back to Brooklyn, and smiling.

Themes to Think About

Choosing vs. Being Chosen For

Nobody forces Eilis to go. But nobody really asks her either. She just... doesn't say no.

Is going along with something the same as choosing it?
Duty to Family

Rose is doing this because she loves Eilis and wants better for her. Eilis, in turn, doesn't want to disappoint Rose or her mother by refusing.

Can loving someone be a reason you stop listening to what you actually want?
Feeling Sad Before Anything Has Even Happened

Eilis hasn't left yet — but there's already a heaviness in how she's living these last days at home.

Can you grieve something before you've even lost it?
Persuasion and Motive

Father Flood's offer sounds generous. But he's not a neutral party making it — he's also the one making the case.

Can an offer still be genuine if the person making it also gains something when you accept it?
Silence and Sacrifice

Rose is giving something up too, quietly. So is Eilis, by not saying what she actually feels. Nobody in this scene says out loud what they're really going through.

Is staying silent to protect someone else its own kind of sacrifice — or just another way of not choosing?
Watching Without Looking

When the new customers come in, almost nobody actually looks at them directly — but everyone is watching. The staff stare and pretend not to; the customers avoid eye contact too, and never come in alone.

Is looking away a form of politeness, or just another way of not dealing with something uncomfortable?
Telling and Not Telling

Eilis writes to Rose about Tony — but leaves out that he's a plumber, and she doesn't tell her mother about him at all. She's still deciding, letter by letter, what each person gets to know.

Is leaving something out the same as lying — or is it something else?
Belonging

In Brooklyn, Eilis starts building something of her own: classes, exams, a boyfriend. But Frank's comment at dinner shows that belonging to one person doesn't mean everyone around them welcomes you.

What does it take to belong somewhere — a place, a person, or both?
Two Homes

Enniscorthy and Brooklyn both pull at Eilis. Back in Ireland, familiar faces and her mother's expectations make Brooklyn feel almost like a dream, until the secret she has been keeping makes it real again.

Can a place feel like home because it is familiar, or does home need something more?
Reputation and Expectations

In Enniscorthy, people notice Eilis, talk about her, and admire her for having come back from America. Her mother's pride and the town's approval make Jim and a life in Ireland look easy to say yes to.

How much do other people's expectations shape what she thinks she wants?
Grief and Duty

Rose's sudden death changes everything. It sends Eilis home, makes her the child her mother needs, and leaves a gap that shapes the whole last part of the book.

When someone is gone, how does what you owe the people who are left change?
An Open Ending

The book stops as Eilis leaves. We never see her arrive back in Brooklyn or meet Tony. Her smile on the train suggests she now understands what her choice means to her, but Tóibín doesn't spell it out.

Why might an author end a story at the moment of choosing, not after it?

The Conflict, Start to Finish

From the beginning, the question is whether Eilis ever really chooses. She went to Brooklyn because Rose and Father Flood arranged it. She says yes to Tony. At home she drifts toward Jim without answering him.

What changes at the end is the secret. Her silence about Tony protected everyone from an uncomfortable conversation, but it also let her avoid deciding. Once Miss Kelly shows she knows, silence is no longer an option, and Eilis acts: she tells her mother, buys her passage, and leaves Jim a note.

What stays open is what she feels and what happens next. The book ends before the reunion, with a smile. Whether she is returning to Tony, to Brooklyn, or to a life that is finally her own is left for you to decide.

Vocabulary

ambivalentfeeling two opposite things about something at the same time — like wanting to stay and wanting to go
reluctantnot really wanting to do something, but doing it anyway
to arrangeto plan or organize something in advance, usually for someone else
to emigrateto leave your own country to go live in another one
opportunitya chance to do something that could improve your life
dutysomething you feel you have to do because of who you are to someone — a daughter, a sister
to overshadowwhen someone else's presence makes you seem less noticeable or less important
resignedaccepting something you don't love, without fighting it — a quiet kind of giving in
tornfeeling pulled in two different directions at once, unable to fully choose one
relievedfeeling lighter because a worry or pressure has eased, even a little
guiltyfeeling responsible for something bad, even if you didn't really cause it on purpose
hopefulexpecting something good might happen, even while other feelings are also present
numbnot really feeling anything — sometimes because there's too much to feel all at once
generouswilling to give more than what's expected — kind in a way that actually costs the giver something
genuinereal and honest, not just appearing that way on the surface
counterargumenta reason or point that pushes back against an argument someone else just made
sacrificegiving up something important of your own so someone else can have or keep something
to articulateto say or explain something clearly, putting a feeling or thought into exact words
passagehere, the journey itself — or the money paid for it, especially for a trip by sea
seasickfeeling sick from the motion of a boat — Eilis has it badly on the way over
homesickmissing home so much it feels physical, not just emotional
immigrationhere, the official process of entering a new country as someone planning to live there
to welcometo invite someone in and treat them as wanted — though the word can be used strategically, not just warmly
motivethe real reason behind a decision, which isn't always the reason that gets said out loud
disapprovala feeling that something is wrong or shouldn't be happening, often shown rather than said
bookkeepingkeeping an organized record of a business's money — what Eilis is training for in her evening classes
tuitionthe money you pay for classes or school
bluntsaying exactly what you think, without softening it — like Frank at dinner
to confideto tell someone something private because you trust them — Eilis confides in Rose about Tony, but not her mother
affectiona warm, caring feeling toward someone
to hold backto stop yourself from saying or doing something you actually feel
to postponeto move something to a later time — Eilis keeps postponing her return to Brooklyn
to concealto hide something on purpose so others don't find out — like Eilis concealing her marriage
to exposeto reveal something that was hidden — Miss Kelly's knowledge threatens to expose Eilis's secret
reputationwhat other people in a community think of you, based on what they see and hear
engagementa formal promise to marry someone; also a period of time before the wedding
griefdeep sadness, especially after someone has died
devotedvery loving and loyal to someone or something
restraintholding back, not showing strong feeling or going too far — also a quality of a writing style that tells big events quietly
ambiguousopen to more than one meaning — not clearly one thing or the other
to driftto move along without a clear plan or decision, carried by events
