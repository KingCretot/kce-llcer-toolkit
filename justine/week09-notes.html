Part 1

Brooklyn — the written exam

One hour · the whole book · questions answered on paper

1. How the paper works

What you know: the exam lasts one hour, covers the entire book, and you answer questions on paper. You do not know exactly what the questions will look like, so prepare for every kind: short factual answers, character questions, theme questions, and one longer answer. If your teacher has shown you a past paper, use it while you practice.

Your plan for the hour

  1. Read the whole paper first. Do this before you write anything. Mark the questions you can answer fastest.
  2. Divide your time. Look at how many questions there are and how many points each one is worth. Give more time to the questions worth more. Write the finishing time for each question in the margin.
  3. Underline the command word. It tells you what job the question wants (see below).
  4. Plan in three lines. For a longer answer, write your main idea and two or three moments from the book before you start the sentences.
  5. Keep five minutes at the end. Read your answers again. Check names, spelling, and that every paragraph answers the question that was asked.

The command words

If the question says……it wants you to
Describesay what happens or what something is like, using details from the book
Explain / Whygive reasons: what happens, and why it happens or why it matters
How does the author show…look at the author's choices (what is said, what is left out, how it is told) and say what effect they have
Analyzetake something apart: look at its pieces and say how each one works
Comparesay what two things have in common and where they differ
To what extent / How fargive an answer that is not all or nothing: "partly", "mostly", "only until…", with reasons on both sides
Discusslook at more than one view, then say where you end up
Illustrate / Give an examplename a precise moment from the book that shows your point
Do you agree?take a position and defend it, and show you have thought about the other side

What a good answer is made of

A strong answer is accurate (the facts are right), precise (it names moments and people, not just "the book"), and explained (it says why the moment matters for the question). Naming a moment without explaining it earns little. Explaining without a moment sounds like opinion.

2. The book in six movements

Brooklyn is by the Irish writer Colm Tóibín (published 2009) and is set in the early 1950s. It is a Bildungsroman, a novel about a young person growing up and finding out who she is. Learn the book as six movements. If you can say each one in two sentences, you can find your way to any question.

1Enniscorthy: a small life and a plan made for her

Eilis lives a limited life in her Irish town, often in the shadow of her sister Rose. There is little work for her. Rose and Father Flood arrange a job and a place to live in Brooklyn. Eilis does not fight the plan, but she does not really choose it either. Her brothers pay for her passage, and the night before she leaves, Rose helps her pack and she realizes Rose is giving up her own future.

Moments to cite: Father Flood presents the offer as simple and generous · Eilis goes along without choosing · the last night with Rose · Rose says nothing about what it costs her.
2The crossing and the arrival

The voyage is rough, and Eilis is seasick for most of it. She shares a cabin with Georgina, a more experienced traveler, who helps her through the worst and prepares her for immigration. In Brooklyn, Father Flood meets her and settles her into Mrs. Kehoe's boarding house with other young Irish women.

Moments to cite: the storm and seasickness · Georgina's help · Father Flood meeting her · the boarding house.
3Brooklyn: work, homesickness, and the first steps toward belonging

Eilis works at Bartocci's department store, trained by women including Miss Fortini. She is homesick, and her letters home leave out how hard it is. The store decides to welcome Black customers, presented as a smart business move. Eilis is one of the assistants chosen to serve them, and every time one comes in the store goes still and watchful. Eilis also goes to evening classes, and passes her first-year exams. A parishioner has paid for her next year of tuition.

Moments to cite: her letters leave out the hard parts · the new customers and the watchful silence · evening classes and exams · the paid tuition.
4Tony: someone who chooses her

At a dance in the parish hall, Tony, a young Italian-American plumber, asks Eilis to dance and walks her home. They go out together, and Tony tells her he loves her and talks about children. Eilis writes to Rose about him but leaves out that he is a plumber, and she does not tell her mother. At dinner with his family, Tony's youngest brother Frank says the family does not like Irish people. There are days at Coney Island and a Dodgers game. Around Christmas, Tony explains that his brothers are building houses on Long Island and hopes one will be for them. Eilis does not answer directly.

Moments to cite: the dance and the walk home · "leaving things out" in her letters · Frank at dinner · Coney Island · the houses on Long Island and her silence.
5Rose's death and the secret marriage

Rose dies suddenly in the night. Father Flood tells Eilis at Bartocci's, and her mother phones that evening and explains that Rose had a heart condition she had hidden from everyone. Eilis's brother Jack writes that she should come home and look after their mother. Overwhelmed, Eilis turns to Tony. Before she goes home for a month, they marry at a simple courthouse ceremony and tell no one.

Moments to cite: Rose's hidden illness · Jack's letter · Eilis turning to Tony · the courthouse wedding that nobody knows about.
6Back in Enniscorthy: the choice

In Ireland, Eilis finds Rose's room untouched and tells nobody about Tony or the marriage. She postpones her return so she can attend Nancy's wedding. She meets Jim Farrell again, and a romance begins. The town admires her, she is offered work in the office where Rose worked, and her mother approves of Jim. After the wedding Jim wants to get engaged, and Eilis does not answer. Then Miss Kelly shows that she knows about the marriage, because Mrs. Kehoe told her. The secret is out. Eilis books her passage, writes to Father Flood, Mrs. Kehoe, and Miss Fortini, and tells her mother she is married. On her last morning she leaves a note at Jim's door promising to explain, and takes the train. The book ends with her imagining Jim hearing that she has gone back to Brooklyn.

Moments to cite: Rose's untouched room · the reputation she has at home · Jim and the engagement she does not answer · Miss Kelly's discovery · telling her mother · the note at Jim's door · the open ending.

3. Characters

For each person, know who they are, what they want, and one moment that shows it.

Eilis LaceyQuiet, observant, and at the start more carried along than choosing. Over the book she starts to decide for herself. Be ready to say how she changes and where she is still unsure.
Rose LaceyHer older sister. Sets the plan going, and quietly gives up her own chance to leave. She hides her illness from everyone. She stands for sacrifice and for silence that protects other people.
Mrs. LaceyTheir mother, a widow. Eilis edits what she tells her. After Rose's death, Jack writes that Eilis should come home and look after her.
Father FloodThe priest who arranges her job and a place to live. He is also the one making the case for America, so his offer is not neutral.
Mrs. KehoeThe landlady. She runs the boarding house where Eilis lives. Her sharing of the secret with Miss Kelly ends Eilis's silence.
Miss FortiniA woman who trains Eilis at Bartocci's. She never looks away during the tense moments at the counter, and Eilis writes to her at the end.
GeorginaThe experienced traveler who shares Eilis's cabin and helps her through the voyage and immigration.
TonyItalian-American plumber. Open about his feelings, patient, loves baseball. He is the first person in Brooklyn who chooses Eilis for himself. He stands for a new life she could build.
FrankTony's youngest brother. Blunt. He says the family does not like Irish people, which shows that being welcomed by one person is not the same as belonging.
JackEilis's brother in England. After Rose's death he writes that Eilis should come home and look after their mother.
NancyEilis's friend in Enniscorthy. Her wedding to George Sheridan is one reason Eilis stays longer than planned, and she helps bring Eilis and Jim together.
Jim FarrellA young man from Enniscorthy. Attentive and a little shy. He stands for the comfortable, approved future at home.
Miss KellyThe shopkeeper Eilis once worked for. Her knowledge of the marriage forces Eilis to act.

4. Themes, with moments to use

For each theme, you have a short statement, three moments, and a question to think about. Learn two or three moments for every theme, because the same moment can serve several questions.

Choosing and being chosen for

Rose and Father Flood arrange Eilis's life, and she goes along without really choosing. By the end she makes real decisions: she tells her mother the truth, books her passage, and leaves.

Moments: Father Flood's offer · Eilis goes along · telling her mother and booking her passage.

Is going along with something the same as choosing it?
Home and belonging

Enniscorthy and Brooklyn both pull at Eilis. Home is familiar, but Brooklyn is where she is building something of her own. Frank's remark shows that belonging to one person does not mean everyone welcomes you.

Moments: homesickness in Brooklyn · Frank at dinner · Enniscorthy making Brooklyn feel like a dream.

Is home a place, a person, or both?
Duty to family and sacrifice

Rose gives up her own chance. Eilis is pulled home by duty after Rose dies. The people who love Eilis often decide what is good for her.

Moments: the last night with Rose · Jack's letter asking Eilis to come home · her mother approving of Jim.

Can loving someone be a reason you stop listening to what you want?
Silence, secrets, and what is left out

Nobody says what they are really going through. Rose hides her illness. Eilis leaves things out of her letters, hides her marriage, and does not answer Tony or Jim. Silence protects other people and also lets Eilis avoid deciding.

Moments: Rose's hidden heart condition · letters that leave things out · the secret marriage and Miss Kelly.

Is leaving something out the same as lying?
Independence and growing up

Because the book is a Bildungsroman, you can follow how Eilis grows: from a quiet girl who is carried along to a woman who sees what her choices mean. She earns her own qualifications, works, loves, and decides.

Moments: evening classes and exams · saying yes to Tony · leaving Jim a note and going back.

At the end, is Eilis in control of her life, or only closer to it?
Migration and being an outsider

Leaving Ireland is a loss as well as an opportunity. Eilis arrives as a stranger and learns the rules of a new place. The same book shows other people who are treated as outsiders.

Moments: the storm and Georgina's help · the Irish women at Mrs. Kehoe's · the Black customers at Bartocci's.

What does it cost to start again somewhere else?
Looking and not looking

At Bartocci's, almost nobody looks at the new customers directly, but everyone is watching. The staff stare and pretend not to, and the customers never come in alone. Miss Fortini does not look away.

Moments: the store going still · "not one ever comes in alone" · Miss Fortini.

Is looking away politeness, or a way of avoiding something uncomfortable?
Reputation and expectations

In Enniscorthy, people notice Eilis and admire her for having come back from America. Her mother's pride and the town's approval make Jim and a life in Ireland look easy to say yes to.

Moments: the town's admiration · the job offer at the office where Rose worked · her mother approving of Jim.

How much do other people's expectations shape what she thinks she wants?
An open ending

The book stops as Eilis leaves. We never see her arrive in Brooklyn or meet Tony. The smile in the last lines suggests she understands her choice, but Tóibín does not spell it out.

Moments: the note at Jim's door · the train · imagining Jim smiling.

Why might an author stop at the moment of choosing rather than after it?

5. How Tóibín tells the story

Some questions ask "how does the author show…". For those, you need to talk about technique as well as events. Here are the tools you can use.

Close third personThe story follows Eilis closely, so we see events through her eyes. We often know what she notices more than what she feels.
RestraintHolding back strong feeling. Big events are told quietly, without much drama. Rose's death and the wedding are told simply. The effect is that the reader has to do some of the feeling.
LettersEilis's letters show a gap between what she lives and what she tells. They are a tool for showing her silence.
Contrast of placesEnniscorthy and Brooklyn are shown side by side, in work, community, and expectations. Going back to Ireland makes Brooklyn look different.
Watching, not sayingMany key scenes, such as Bartocci's, rely on looks and silence rather than speech.
The endingThe book ends before the reunion, with an image rather than an explanation.
Sentence frames for technique: "Tóibín uses ___ to show ___." · "By telling this scene quietly, he makes the reader ___." · "The gap between what Eilis feels and what she writes suggests ___." · "The ending leaves it open whether ___."

6. Building an answer

The four moves: Point · Evidence · Explain · Link

  1. Point. One sentence that answers the question directly.
  2. Evidence. A precise moment from the book: who, where, what happens.
  3. Explain. Say what the moment shows and why it supports your point. This is the move that earns the points.
  4. Link. Tie it back to the question or to the next idea, so the paragraph does not end in the middle of the story.

A worked model, with a different document

To show the four moves without giving you a Brooklyn answer to memorize, here is a paragraph about a painting that is not part of your course: Nighthawks, by the American painter Edward Hopper (1942).

Question: How does Hopper create a feeling of loneliness in Nighthawks?

Point: Hopper creates loneliness by placing people close together but making them look separate. Evidence: Four people are in a brightly lit diner at night, and none of them is looking at anyone else. Explain: Because they share the same small space and still do not meet each other's eyes, the picture suggests that being near people is not the same as being connected to them. Link: The dark, empty street outside makes the lit diner feel even more cut off, which strengthens the feeling.

Notice what the paragraph does not do: it does not describe everything in the painting, and every sentence points back to the question.

Language you can use

Starting a point: "One way Tóibín shows this is…" · "The clearest example is…" · "This is most obvious when…"
Introducing evidence: "For example, when Eilis…" · "This happens when…" · "In the scene where…"
Explaining: "This shows that…" · "This suggests…" · "The effect is that…" · "In other words…"
Adding: "Moreover…" · "In addition…" · "What is more…"
Contrasting: "However…" · "On the other hand…" · "Although…" · "Whereas…"
Qualifying (for "to what extent"): "To some extent…" · "Mostly, but not completely…" · "Only until…"
Concluding: "Overall…" · "In the end…" · "This is why…"

Verbs for talking about the book

reveals · suggests · shows · implies · highlights · contrasts · emphasizes · conceals · hides · struggles · hesitates · resists · gives in · confides · holds back · chooses · is carried along · feels torn.

Longer answers: a shape

For a longer answer, write a short introduction (one or two sentences that answer the question), two or three paragraphs each built with Point–Evidence–Explain–Link, and a short conclusion that gives your overall view. For "to what extent", one paragraph can give the case for, one the case against, and the conclusion says where you stand.

Do and do not

  • Do use the present tense to talk about the book: "Eilis goes to Brooklyn", not "went".
  • Do use names: "Father Flood", "Miss Kelly", "Tony".
  • Do not tell the plot again for a page. Choose the moment that fits the question.
  • Do not write "the author wants to say that" and then leave it there. Say what the moment shows.
  • If you forget a name, describe the person: "the shopkeeper in Enniscorthy", and move on.

If the paper gives you an extract

If the paper includes a passage, start by saying where it falls in the book and what has just happened. Then look closely at two or three details in the passage (what is said, what is left unsaid, a word that stands out). Finally, link out: say how the moment connects to a theme or to a change in Eilis. Do not tell the whole book again. Stay close to the passage, then widen.

AvoidSay instead

2. Your documents, one by one

These are the documents from your class that fit this theme. For each, you have a short fact sheet and two ideas: how it shows expression and construction of the self, and how it can connect to Brooklyn. These are ideas to test, not answers to copy. Pick the ones you can talk about best.

Brooklyn
Colm Tóibín · novel · 2009 · full work (Œuvre intégrale)

What it is: a Bildungsroman about Eilis, a young Irish woman who emigrates to Brooklyn, builds a life there, and has to choose between two homes.

Expression and construction: Eilis builds herself by working, studying, and loving, but she often does not say what she feels. Her letters show her choosing what each person gets to know.

Good for: identity under pressure from family and place; secrets; choosing and being chosen for.

Jane Eyre, Chapter IV (the confrontation with Mrs. Reed)
Charlotte Brontë · novel · 1847 · literary text

What it is: Jane, a ten-year-old orphan living with her aunt Mrs. Reed, hears her aunt describe her to Mr. Brocklehurst, the headmaster of the school she is to attend, as deceitful. Jane can no longer stay silent, and she confronts her aunt.

Class question: Can expressing one's emotions become an act of liberation?

What the class looked at: the shift in power; anger and suffering, then happiness and freedom; direct speech, exclamations, and emphatic language; the metaphor of an invisible bond that bursts.

Expression and construction: speaking out is how Jane starts to define herself. She stops being "defined by others".

Link to Eilis: both are young women who want some independence and do not want to be controlled. Be careful: Jane explodes in one scene, while Eilis changes slowly and often stays silent. The contrast is as useful as the similarity.

"Eveline" (from Dubliners)
James Joyce · short story · 1914 · literary text

What it is: Eveline, a young woman in Dublin, lives with her father and works in a shop. Her mother is dead, and she has taken on many family responsibilities. One evening, at the window, she thinks about her childhood and about leaving. A young sailor, Frank, has promised her a new life in Buenos Aires. She has to choose between the fear of the known and an uncertain future abroad.

Class question: Why is it sometimes so difficult to break free from one's past?

What the class looked at: the contrast between past and present; freedom against duty; memories and the dilemma; the use of "used to".

Expression and construction: Eveline cannot express what she wants, and so she cannot build a different life.

Link to Eilis: the situation is almost the same (leaving home and family for a new life), and the outcome is the opposite. Use the contrast.

Self-Portrait Dedicated to Leon Trotsky (also called Between the Curtains)
Frida Kahlo · oil on hard board (Masonite) · 1937 · visual art · National Museum of Women in the Arts, Washington, D.C.

What it is: Kahlo stands between two white curtains as if on a stage. She holds a letter to Trotsky and a bunch of flowers. She gave the painting to Trotsky for his birthday on November 7, 1937.

What to say about the artist: born in Mexico in 1907; polio as a child; a serious bus accident at eighteen that kept her in bed for months, when a mirror was placed above her bed so she could paint herself. Your class notes say 55 of her roughly 143 paintings are self-portraits. She rejected the Surrealist label and said she painted her own reality.

Expression and construction: the curtains make her portrait a staging. The Mexican jewelry and clothes, the flowers, and her steady gaze are choices she makes to present herself.

Link to Brooklyn: both Kahlo and Eilis decide how to present themselves in front of people who are watching (Kahlo to the viewer, Eilis in letters and in front of Enniscorthy).

Check with your class notes: your group studied this painting, but confirm which works your group presented and use those first.

Untitled Film Still #12 (and the series Untitled Film Stills)
Cindy Sherman · gelatin silver print · 1978 · visual art · series made between 1977 and 1980

What it is: a black-and-white photograph from a series in which Sherman poses as generic women from 1950s and 1960s movies (Hollywood, dark crime films called film noir, cheap B movies, European art films). Check the print in your own notes before you describe it.

Key facts: the series has seventy photographs. The characters are not real film stills. Sherman is both the artist and the model. The museum that owns the series says they are not considered self-portraits.

Expression and construction: identity is shown as costume, setting, and pose: a self put together from roles that society expects. Sherman has said there are "so many levels of artifice" and that she likes the "jumble of ambiguity".

Link to Brooklyn: Eilis is a different person in each place (the new arrival, the shop girl, the wife, the returning success). If your still includes a suitcase or a doorway, that can link to leaving and arriving.

Check with your class notes: if your group studied a different still, use that one, and cite it as "Untitled Film Still" plus its number.

Vivian Maier and David Hockney: self-portraits
textbook unit "Disclosing the creative process" · visual art

What it is: two self-portraits in your textbook. Maier's is dated 1956 and shows her with her camera and her reflection. The class task was to describe one, present it to a partner, then compare how each artist stages the self.

Expression and construction: both artists put the tools or the setting of their art in the picture (the camera, the studio). Look for what each one shows of themselves and what they keep back.

3. The problématique

The problématique is the central question of your dossier. All your documents have to help answer it. It is the line that turns a pile of documents into a dossier.

What makes a good one

  • It is a question, not a topic. "Identity in literature" is a topic. "How can…?" is a question.
  • It is open. There is more than one possible answer, so your documents can discuss it. A yes-or-no question usually closes too fast.
  • It fits the theme. It uses the words of expression and construction de soi, or their ideas (express, hide, build, become, choose).
  • Each document can help answer it, in its own way. If a document does not fit, change the document or the question.
  • It is yours. You can say why it matters to you in a sentence.

Four steps to build it

  1. Choose three or four documents that you can talk about confidently.
  2. Find what they share. Ask: what is each document really about, in one sentence? Where do two documents agree? Where do they disagree?
  3. Turn the shared idea into a question, using one of the openers below.
  4. Test it. Say the question and answer it with each document in one sentence. If each document gives a different angle, the question works.

Question openers

"How can…?" · "To what extent…?" · "In what ways…?" · "Can we…?" · "Is it possible to…?" · "Why is it so hard to…?" · "Does…or…?" · "What does it take to…?"

Weak and strong: an example with another theme

To show you the difference without giving you an answer, here is a pair on a different theme, myths and heroes.

WeakStronger
"Heroes in stories.""Do we need heroes to feel brave ourselves?"
"Is Superman a hero?""How do stories turn ordinary people into heroes, and what do they leave out?"

The weak ones are a topic, or a question with only one answer. The stronger ones are open, can be discussed with different documents, and suggest an angle.

Making the connection out loud

"Both ___ and ___ show that ___, but in different ways. In ___, ___. In ___, ___."
"If you read ___ next to ___, you can see that ___."
"___ answers my question by showing ___, while ___ complicates it by ___."

5. Presenting on Thursday

On Thursday you present your dossier to your teacher. You do not know exactly how long you will have. Prepare a short version (about three minutes is one possible length) and be ready to be asked questions. Speak from a few key words, not from a full text.

A shape for a short version

  1. Introduce the dossier. Name the theme, say your central question, and say which documents you chose.
  2. First document. What it is, then how it starts to answer your question.
  3. Second document. What it is, and how it gives a different angle.
  4. Third document, and your personal document. Show how it adds to or complicates the others.
  5. Answer your question. Say where you end up, in two or three sentences.
Opening: "My dossier is on the theme of the expression and construction of the self. My central question is: ___. To answer it, I chose ___."
Introducing a document: "My first document is ___ by ___. It is a ___ from ___."
Linking to the question: "This is relevant to my question because ___."
Moving on: "A different angle appears in ___, where ___."
Concluding: "To answer my question, I would say that ___, although ___."

Bringing Brooklyn in

Your class has linked each literary text to Eilis in Brooklyn. You can do the same in the dossier: use a short link ("this reminds me of Eilis when…") to show that you know the full work, and keep the long discussion for the documents you chose.

Questions your teacher might ask

  • Why did you choose this question?
  • Which document is the most important for your question, and why?
  • Where do two of your documents disagree?
  • What does Brooklyn add to your question?
  • Why did you choose your personal document, and how does it fit?
  • Is there a document in your dossier that does not fit well? What would you change?

Think about each one in advance and have a short answer, with an example, ready.
