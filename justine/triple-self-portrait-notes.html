Triple Self-Portrait — Your Notes

Norman Rockwell, 1960 — and the theme of expressing and building yourself

These notes are for this one document. Use them to check what you noticed, find words for what you want to say, and see how the pieces fit together. They do not tell you what to say about it, and they do not tell you why to choose it. That part is yours.

Norman Rockwell, Triple Self-Portrait, 1960. Image: Norman Rockwell Museum.

The Document

TitleTriple Self-Portrait
ArtistNorman Rockwell, an American painter and illustrator, best known for his covers for The Saturday Evening Post.
WhenIt appeared on the cover of the Post in February 1960.
Kind of workAn oil painting. It was made as a magazine cover, so it was meant to be seen by a very large audience.
Why it fitsIt is an American work, and it is about a painter looking at himself. That makes it a direct fit for the theme of expressing yourself and building your own image.

What to Look At

1

The painter, from behind. We see Rockwell's back as he sits in front of his canvas. We are looking over his shoulder, as if we were in the room.

2

The reflection. He is looking into a mirror. The face in the mirror is the one he is studying in order to paint himself.

3

The portrait on the canvas. This is the version he is making. Compare it carefully with the reflection. Ask yourself what he kept, what he changed, and why.

4

The pinned portraits. Around the top of the canvas are self-portraits by famous artists, including Dürer, Rembrandt, Picasso, and Van Gogh. They are small, but they matter: Rockwell has put himself among the artists who are best known for painting themselves.

5

The mood. It is serious and playful at the same time. Notice how a very simple scene (a man painting) makes you ask a big question (which one is the real him?).

Ideas That Connect to the Theme

Expression and Construction

Any self-portrait does two things at once. It expresses the artist (this is how I see myself), and it constructs an image (this is how I decide to show myself). Rockwell puts both on the same canvas.

Is there a difference between showing who you are and deciding who to show?
Three Selves

The man from behind, the face in the mirror, and the face on the canvas are three versions of one person. None of them lets us look him in the eye directly.

Can anyone ever see themselves completely?
Mirror and Canvas

A mirror shows you what is there. A canvas shows what the painter chooses. In daily life we are often somewhere between the two, especially when we know others are watching.

When you present yourself to others, which do you resemble more?
Standing Among the Masters

By pinning up famous self-portraits, Rockwell shows he knows the tradition he is joining. It can read as respect, as ambition, or as humor.

What does he gain by putting himself next to those artists?

Your Recording in Three Parts

Your task asks for three things. Plan each one with just a few keywords in your head, or on paper if you like.

1. Introduce the document. Say who made it, what it is, and when. Add where you found it or why people know it.

2. Say what it represents, and link it to the theme. Say what it shows, name the idea about expressing or building yourself, and point to one detail that proves your point.

3. Say why you chose it. This one only you can answer. A good reason is specific: something you noticed, something it made you think about, or something about you.

Tips for speaking from notes: keep only keywords, say the whole thing out loud more than once, and let the words change each time. If you lose your place, glance at your card, find the next keyword, and keep going. A short pause is fine.
