PRIVATE — TUTOR GUIDE. This is for you, not Justine. It contains talking points and a "what good sounds like" guide that the lesson is built to make her find on her own.
ASSIGNMENT NOTE: this week is a workshop, not a reading lesson. Justine's class assignment is a portfolio piece for the theme expression et construction de soi: she must choose a personal document (a painting, drawing, sculpture, song, or an extract from a book or play) linked to the English-speaking world, upload it to the class digital wall with a recording, and in the recording introduce the document, explain what it represents in relation to the theme, and say why she chose it. She must speak spontaneously from notes, not read. She chose Norman Rockwell's Triple Self-Portrait and has not yet worked on it. The lesson helps her look, find ideas, form her own reading of the painting, and practice speaking. It does not write her recording, and it cannot supply her reason for choosing the painting. That reason has to come from her.

Week 8 — Tutor Guide

Everything you need to run the portfolio workshop without being an art expert.

Ask Justine Before You Start

Things we do not know yet
  • What is the digital wall (which platform), and is the upload due on a specific date?
  • How long should the recording be? Did her teacher give a minimum or a maximum?
  • Did her teacher approve Triple Self-Portrait, or was approval not needed?
  • Did the teacher say anything about how the work will be marked (language, content, spontaneity)?
  • Does she plan to appear on camera, or is it audio only?
  • Is the Brooklyn evaluation still ahead of her, and has her teacher said when and in what format? She mentioned one after the break. This week's session may be a good moment to ask.
  • Why this painting? Ask early, but gently, and do not offer an answer. If she says "I don't know" or "I just liked it", that is a normal starting point. The Follow-Up stop and the Notes page give her ways to find a more specific reason.
Your role in this lesson

Coach, not author. Let her look and speak first, before you show her anything. Correct language lightly (a short fix in the moment, then move on) and save bigger corrections for after she has finished a full run-through. Do not hand her sentences to memorize. She needs her own words, in her own order. If she goes quiet, ask a question rather than give an answer.

Suggested Flow

  1. Open (a few minutes): she draws a topic and talks. The topics all touch on how we see ourselves, which sets up the theme without naming it.
  2. Look First: she describes the painting cold, with no help. Listen for vocabulary she needs and does not have. Then use the check box to fill in the facts.
  3. What It Means: she works through the idea cards, then finishes the sentence "Rockwell's painting suggests that expressing who you are and building an image of yourself are..." and defends it with one detail. This is the main work of the session, so give it the most time. Nothing is typed.
  4. Follow-Up: five questions. The first, "Why did you choose this document?", is the most important. If her answer is vague, ask her to try again with something she noticed in the painting.
  5. Range Practice: five short rounds with art-description techniques. These should feel quick and a bit playful.
  6. Argue & Run It: the coin gives her a side, then she runs the three-part recording talk from start to finish (three keywords for each part, said aloud). If time is short, shorten Argue It, but do not skip the run. The run is the assignment.
  7. Final run-through: if there is time, she says her position in two sentences, then delivers the three-part talk once, with no notes and no reading. Give feedback on what worked first, then one or two things to change. She should then record it herself, on her own.

What We Have Verified About the Painting

Safe to say to Justine
  • Artist: Norman Rockwell, American painter and illustrator, best known for his covers for The Saturday Evening Post. He worked with the magazine for decades, from 1916 into the 1960s.
  • Work: Triple Self-Portrait, oil on canvas, published on the cover of the Post in February 1960.
  • Scene: Rockwell is seen from behind, sitting in front of a canvas and looking into a mirror. The picture contains three versions of him: the figure from behind, the reflection in the mirror, and the portrait he is painting.
  • Pinned portraits: self-portraits by famous artists are pinned to the top of the canvas, including Dürer, Rembrandt, Picasso, and Van Gogh.
  • Differences between the faces: sources describe the portrait on the canvas as more flattering or idealized than the reflection. Let her judge this for herself first, and treat the sources as confirmation, not as the answer.
Check these — the sources disagree or are thin
  • 1959 or 1960? The museum dates the painting 1959 and the cover to February 13, 1960. The lesson and Notes say "painted in 1959, published in 1960". If her teacher or another source gives a different date, use hers.
  • Number of pinned self-portraits. Sources give different counts. Do not ask her to count them. The four names above are the ones that are consistently mentioned.
  • What is visible in the image. A pipe is in the mouth of all three versions. A brass fireman's-style helmet sits on top of the easel. An eagle tops the mirror's gold frame. In the mirror, the glasses are blank white discs, so his eyes are hidden (the Saturday Evening Post article says glare kept him from seeing his own eyes). The figure from behind shows only the arm of his glasses. The canvas face has no glasses and clear eyes. At the upper right of the canvas, Dürer, Rembrandt, Picasso, and Van Gogh are pinned as small reproductions; a separate sheet of face sketches is pinned at the left. Let her notice these herself.
  • Extra details from the Post and museum articles (use only if she asks). The helmet is described as a French fireman's helmet Rockwell bought in Paris. The eagle on the frame was added; his real studio mirror did not have it. The Post says Rockwell could not see how he looked in the glare, so he could "stretch the truth just a bit". This is a paraphrase from the Post, not a verbatim quote.
  • Where it is now. The Norman Rockwell Museum in Stockbridge, Massachusetts (Norman Rockwell Art Collection Trust).
  • What the title means. "Triple" usually refers to the three versions in the picture, but this is an interpretation. The lesson presents it as one reading.
  • (Superseded by the Post note above.) A remark attributed to Rockwell about stretching the truth or flattering himself in the portrait appears in only one source I found. Do not present it to Justine as something he said.
  • Where the painting is now. I did not confirm its current location for the lesson. If she wants to say where it can be seen, she should check the Norman Rockwell Museum's website.
  • Theme definition. I could not reach the official text for expression et construction de soi. The lesson uses the plain-language reading ("how people express who they are and how they build an image of themselves"), based on a published summary of the LLCER themes. Ask her what her teacher said the theme means. Her teacher's version takes priority.

Stop 1 — Open

Task

Six everyday prompts about how we see ourselves and how others see us. No answer key. A full-sentence answer for about a minute is a good result.

Stop 2 — Look First

Task

A cold description of the painting. Get her to describe before she interprets. The five prompts take her from the facts (who, where, how many) to the mood.

What a complete description includes
  • A man sits with his back to us, in front of a canvas, and looks into a mirror.
  • There are three versions of him: from behind, in the mirror, and on the canvas.
  • The portraits of other artists are pinned up around the canvas.
  • Foreground and background: the painter and the canvas are the focus, with the mirror and the pinned pictures framing the scene.
  • Mood: she can say serious, humorous, thoughtful, self-mocking, calm, or something else, as long as she backs it up with something she sees.
Language to listen for
  • Does she use "in the picture", "in the foreground", "behind him"? Common slips: "on the picture" (should be "in"), "from the back" (works but "from behind" is more natural), "the painter makes a portrait of him" (should be "paints").
  • Pronunciation worth a quick check (approximate): portrait = PORE-trit, mirror = MEER-er, canvas = CAN-vus, Dürer = DUR-er, Van Gogh = van GO (American). Do not make a big deal of any of them.

Stop 3 — What It Means

Task

Six idea cards, then a position sentence. The cards give her angles, and the position sentence is where she commits to an interpretation. Nothing is typed. She talks and you listen. The goal is that she leaves with a view she can say in two sentences and defend with a detail from the painting.

What to listen for on each card
  • Three selves: she notices that we never see his real face straight on, only the back view, the reflection, and the portrait on the canvas. A strong answer says that "which one is real?" is the point of the painting, or that no single version is complete.
  • The mirror: the mirror as honesty, or as self-judgment. Any personal example is a plus, but do not push for one.
  • The canvas: the portrait as a choice. She should compare the canvas with the reflection and notice something specific.
  • The masters: respect, ambition, humor, or all three. Rockwell was an illustrator, and placing himself next to these artists can be read as bold, funny, or modest. All are acceptable as long as she explains.
  • The magazine cover: the audience matters. A self-portrait on a magazine cover is public in a way that a private sketch is not.
  • Why a self-portrait?: the expression and construction distinction. This is the closest link to her theme.
The position sentence: three readings (interpretations, not facts)
  • They are the same thing. To express yourself is already to choose how. Supporting details: the canvas is a choice, the angle is a choice, the masters are a choice.
  • They pull against each other. The built image can hide the person. Supporting details: we never see his real face straight on, the canvas version looks more flattering than the reflection, it is a public cover.
  • You can never see yourself completely. Supporting details: three versions, none complete; he needs a mirror to see himself at all.

Any of these, a mix, or her own reading is fine. What matters: (1) she states it in her own words, (2) she supports it with something visible in the painting, (3) she can say what someone who disagrees would point to. If her statement is vague ("it shows identity"), ask: "What about identity? What is the painting saying about it?" Do not choose a reading for her. The lesson shows the three readings only after she has tried her own sentence. If she is stuck, try: "What is the first thing you notice?", "Which of the three Rockwells would you trust?", or "Can you see his eyes in the mirror? What does that suggest?"

The "Look Again" questions
  • He looks at the mirror, not at us. We stand behind him, so we see what he sees only through the reflection.
  • We see the back of the figure and the faces in the mirror and canvas, but never his real face straight on. The canvas face is shown frontally, but it is the portrait he is making, not his real face.
  • Differences between reflection and canvas are for her to find. Sources call the canvas version more flattering; confirm only after she has looked.
  • The masters: any reasoned answer.

Stop 4 — Follow-Up

Task

Five bilingual questions. They have no right answers. Listen for specifics and for reasons that begin with "because".

What to listen for
  • Why this document? This question is always asked first in the lesson. The most important question. A strong answer is specific (a detail she noticed, an idea she connected with, a feeling it gave her). A weak answer is "it's famous" or "I found it online". If she gives a weak answer, ask: "What's the first thing you noticed when you saw it?" and let her try again.
  • Your own self-portrait: she needs to balance what she would show and leave out. This question connects the painting to her own experience, which will help in the "why I chose it" section.
  • Most honest version: there is no single answer. Accept any choice with a reason. A sophisticated answer is "none of them" or "the one in the mirror, because the other two are decisions".
  • Others' view vs your own: personal. Do not push if she prefers to keep it general.
  • Social media: a bridge to the modern world. Good answers recognize that profiles are curated (like the canvas), but can also be a way to express oneself honestly.

Stop 5 — Range Practice

Task

Five rounds of one sentence and one technique each: a different verb; "While..."; "seems to" or "as if"; a casual register; "Instead of...". All examples are sample answers. She does not need to match them. Accept any version that uses the technique correctly.

Stop 6 — Argue & Run It

Task

"Does Rockwell show us who he really is — or who he wants us to see?" The coin assigns a side. Both sides can be argued well from what is visible in the painting. A strong answer, whichever side she gets, uses at least two details she can point to. Bonus for acknowledging the other view at the end.

FOR — he shows who he really is

  • He shows himself from behind, in the act of working, rather than as a polished hero.
  • He includes the mirror, which shows the version he is actually looking at.
  • He places himself next to the great masters, which could be read as modest or humorous. It invites comparison with artists who are far more famous for self-portraits.
  • By showing three versions, he is honest that no portrait is complete.
  • The playfulness may itself be who he is: an illustrator known for warmth and humor.

AGAINST — he shows who he wants us to see

  • He controls the composition: he chooses the angle, and we never see his real face straight on.
  • The portrait on the canvas is a choice, described by sources as more flattering than the reflection.
  • He chooses which artists to pin up, which curates his image and links him with famous names.
  • It is a magazine cover, made for millions of readers, so it is a public image, not a private confession.
  • The cleverness of the idea can itself be a performance.
A strong "both" move

Either side can finish by saying that the painting shows how he constructs himself, and that this may be the most honest thing about it. That is exactly the link to expression et construction de soi, so it is a good point to hold in reserve for her recording.

Mistakes French Speakers Often Make Here

  • "on the picture" instead of "in the picture"; "from the back" instead of "from behind".
  • "makes a portrait" instead of "paints a portrait"; "looks himself" instead of "looks at himself".
  • "the public" or "the personage" instead of "the viewer" and "the man"; "reflexion"; "actually" meaning "currently"; "sensible" meaning "sensitive".
  • "tableau" as a false friend for "table": say "painting".

What a Strong Recording Sounds Like

This is for you only. Use it to judge a practice run, not to give her a script.

Three parts, in order
  • Introduction: it names the artist, the title, and the date or the context, and says what kind of work it is. It takes about the first fifteen to thirty seconds.
  • What it represents and the link to the theme: it begins with a description (what is in the painting, where), moves to an interpretation (what it suggests about expressing and building yourself), and ties the two together with at least one detail. It names the theme in her own words.
  • Why she chose it: it is personal and specific. It could be something she noticed, a question it raised, or a connection to her own life. It sounds like her and not like a general statement.
Sounds like speaking, not reading
  • Natural pace and short pauses, not a flat, fast stream.
  • Sentences that change slightly on each take.
  • Small repairs ("I mean...", "let me say that differently") are normal and fine.
  • If she begins to sound like she is reciting, ask her to look away from the card and try again.

Likely Questions From Her Teacher or Classmates

  • Why did you choose this document?
  • How does it relate to the theme of expression et construction de soi?
  • What does the mirror represent?
  • Why do you think he put other artists' self-portraits in the picture?
  • Which of the three versions of Rockwell is the most truthful?
  • Could you imagine doing something similar for yourself?
