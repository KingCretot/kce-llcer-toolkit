Brooklyn · Road 4 — Tutor Guide

Everything you need to run the lesson without having read the book yourself.

What Happens Between Road 3 and Road 4

Father Flood's offer (Road 3) doesn't stay abstract — it turns into paperwork and packing quickly. Eilis's brothers (working in Birmingham) agree to pay for her passage; Rose handles the sponsorship documents. About a month before she leaves, Eilis tells her boss, Miss Kelly, that she's going — and Miss Kelly fires her on the spot rather than keeping her on for the remaining weeks. Eilis spends her last weeks at home with her friend Nancy, who's just starting to date a well-liked local man named George Sheridan.

The scene this week's Road is actually built around: the night before Eilis leaves, Rose helps her pack and gives her jewelry and money. During this, Eilis has a sudden realization — Rose isn't just being generous. By making this trip possible for Eilis, Rose is giving up her own shot at leaving: she'll be the one who stays home to look after their mother, which almost certainly means Rose won't marry either. Eilis says nothing about any of this to Rose. She decides it would only upset her, since Rose and their mother have already committed to doing this for her.

Stop 2 — Getting Ready to Leave

Task

The student lists what actually happens to prepare for the departure, and what Eilis realizes about Rose during the packing scene.

What actually happens — verified
  • Eilis's brothers agree to pay for her passage (the fare for the voyage); Rose takes care of the sponsorship paperwork.
  • The night before she leaves, Rose helps her pack, and gives her jewelry and money.
  • During this, Eilis realizes Rose is sacrificing her own future — staying behind with their mother, likely giving up her own chance to marry — to make this possible for Eilis.
  • Eilis says nothing about it. She decides speaking up would only upset Rose and her mother.
What a strong answer looks like

Names the practical facts (passage, paperwork) and the realization about Rose — not just one or the other. If she only lists logistics, prompt with: "What does Eilis figure out about Rose that night, specifically?"

Stop 3 — What It Costs

Task

She holds two things at once: Rose's quiet sacrifice, and the fact that Eilis still says nothing about her own doubts.

No single correct answer here either

Same design as last week's synthesis stop — don't push toward one conclusion. The strongest answers hold both truths: staying silent can genuinely be protective, and it can also just be another way of avoiding a real conversation. Both at once, not one or the other.

Stop 4 & Stop 5

Same as Road 3 — Stop 4 is personal-reaction only (no answer key needed), Stop 5 is pure language drill with its own model examples built into the reveal button. Nothing extra needed from you for either.

Stop 6 — Argue It

Task

Coin decides her side on: "Was Eilis right to stay silent about her doubts — or should she have told Rose and her mother the truth before it was too late?"

FOR — staying silent was right

  • The arrangements were already made — speaking up might only have added guilt without changing anything practical.
  • Rose and her mother had already decided together to do this for her; pushing back could look ungrateful for a real sacrifice.
  • Going along quietly can be its own form of love — protecting people from a truth that wouldn't help anything.
  • Eilis is young and has never left home — her own doubts might not even be something she trusts yet, so caution isn't the same as cowardice.

AGAINST — she should have spoken up

  • Rose is making an enormous, life-altering sacrifice without ever hearing how Eilis actually feels about any of it — that's not fully fair to Rose.
  • Silence doesn't protect anyone here — it just delays the moment the truth comes out, by which point it may be too late to do anything differently.
  • If Eilis genuinely doesn't want to go, staying silent guarantees she goes anyway. Speaking up is the only version of events where her own wishes get a real chance to matter.
  • This is now the third time in a row she's stayed quiet (not fighting the original plan, not questioning Father Flood, now not telling Rose). Worth raising if the student's argument leans this way: does the pattern make it harder to break each time?
